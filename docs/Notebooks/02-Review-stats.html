Review of Statistics and Probability

Sunil Paul

(Reference: Stock and Watson) *** ## Some definitions

Sample Space (\(S\))
The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment.

Outcome (or Event)
A subset of the sample space representing mutually exclusive potential results of a random process.
- Example 1: Your grade on an exam.
- Example 2: While attending an online class, the wireless connection might never fail, fail once, fail twice, and so on.

Probability (\(P\))
Captures the idea of “chance”. The probability of an event \(A\) is denoted \(P(A)\).

Kolmogorov’s Three Axioms of Probability

  1. Non-negativity:
    \[ P(A) \geq 0 \quad \text{for any event } A \subseteq S \]

  2. Normalization:
    \[ P(S) = 1 \]

  3. Additivity (Disjoint Events):
    If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive events (\(A \cap B = \varnothing\)), then
    \[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \]

Axiom III and Joint Probability

Axiom III applies when events are disjoint—that is, they cannot happen together.
For non-disjoint events, the general addition rule uses joint probability:
\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

  • Here, \(P(A \cap B)\) is the joint probability: the probability that both \(A\) and \(B\) occur together.
  • If \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint, \(P(A \cap B) = 0\), and the formula reduces to Axiom III

Conditional Probability

The conditional probability of event \(A\) (forward looking probability)given that event \(B\) has occurred is denoted by: \[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad \text{provided } P(B) > 0 \]

  • \(P(A \cap B)\) is the joint probability (both \(A\) and \(B\) happen).
  • \(P(B)\) is the marginal probability of \(B\).
  • This formula adjusts the probability of \(A\) to reflect the knowledge that \(B\) has already occurred.

Example:
If 30% of students play football (\(P(F) = 0.3\)),
and 10% of students play both football and cricket (\(P(F \cap C) = 0.1\)),
then the probability that a football player also plays cricket is: \[ P(C \mid F) = \frac{0.1}{0.3} \approx 0.33 \]

Bayes’ Theorem

Statement:
Bayes’ theorem relates conditional and marginal probabilities, allowing us to update beliefs in light of new evidence.

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}=\frac{P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)} \]

Where: - \(P(B)\) = Prior probability of \(B\).
- \(P(A \mid B)\) = Likelihood: probability of observing \(A\) if \(B\) is true.
- \(P(A)\) = Marginal probability of \(A\): \[ P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i) \cdot P(B_i) \] for all mutually exclusive and exhaustive events \(B_i\).

Note: By the law of total probability
\[ P(A) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) + P(A \mid B') \cdot P(B') \] where \(B'\) is the complement of \(B\) —

Example (Medical Test):**

  • \(P(D)\): Probability a patient has the disease = 0.01.
  • \(P(\text{Positive} \mid D)\): Test is positive if disease is present = 0.99.
  • \(P(\text{Positive} \mid \neg D)\): Test is positive if disease is absent = 0.05.

We want \(P(D \mid \text{Positive})\).

First, find \(P(\text{Positive})\): \[ P(\text{Positive}) = P(\text{Positive} \mid D)P(D) + P(\text{Positive} \mid \neg D)P(\neg D) \] \[ = 0.99(0.01) + 0.05(0.99) = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594 \]

Then, apply Bayes’ theorem: \[ P(D \mid \text{Positive}) = \frac{0.99 \cdot 0.01}{0.0594} \approx 0.1667 \]

Interpretation:
Even with a highly accurate test, the probability of having the disease given a positive result is only ~16.7%, due to the low base rate (\(P(D)\)).

Example 2.4 — Contact Tracing and Self-Quarantine Study

A scientist is studying methods to slow the spread of an RNA-based virus that causes fever. Using contact tracing, they collect data on patients to identify: 1. The patient, 2. The mode of transmission, 3. The infection duration, 4. The self-isolation or self-quarantine period, 5. The role of asymptomatic individuals, who can spread the virus but are harder to identify.

From 59 patients surveyed: - 15 patients did not know how they became infected. - 44 patients knew the source of their infection.

Let: - \(A\): “Known source of infection” - \(A'\): “Unknown source of infection”

Thus: \[ P(A) = \frac{44}{59} \approx 0.75,\quad P(A') = \frac{15}{59} \approx 0.25 \]

It was also found that: - 43 of the 44 patients who knew the source \((A)\) self-quarantined for 14 days. - 6 of the 15 patients with unknown source (\(A'\)) did not self-quarantine.

Let: - \(Q\): “Patient self-quarantined” - \(Q'\): “Patient did not self-quarantine”

We can calculate: - \[ P(Q|A) = \frac{43}{44} \approx 0.98 \] - \[ P(Q|A') = 1 - \frac{6}{15} = 0.6 \]

We also find: \[ P(A' \cap Q) = P(A') \times P(Q|A') = 0.25 \times 0.6 = 0.15 \]

Finally, the scientist is interested in:

\(P(A'|Q)\) = Probability that a patient did not know the source of infection given they self-quarantined

This requires Bayes’ theorem and the law of total probability.

Excercises

1) Credit Risk

A bank lends to two borrower types: - Low-risk borrowers occur with probability \(P(L)=0.70\). Their default rate is \(P(D\mid L)=0.02\). - High-risk borrowers occur with probability \(P(H)=0.30\). Their default rate is \(P(D\mid H)=0.10\).

Tasks 1. Draw the full probability tree (first split by borrower type, then by default/no-default). 2. Compute the overall default probability \(P(D)\). 3. Using Bayes’ theorem, find the posterior probability that a defaulter is high-risk: \(P(H\mid D)\).

2) Market Research (Product Returns)

A firm sells in two regions: - Urban market share \(P(U)=0.60\); return rate \(P(R\mid U)=0.05\). - Rural market share \(P(Ru)=0.40\); return rate \(P(R\mid Ru)=0.12\).

Tasks 1. Draw the full probability tree (first split by region, then by return/no-return). 2. Compute the overall return probability \(P(R)\). 3. Using Bayes’ theorem, find the probability a returned product came from the rural market: \(P(Ru\mid R)\).

3) Monetary Policy Forecast

Historically, the central bank raises rates with probability \(P(H)=0.30\) (no hike \(H'\) with \(0.70\).
Analyst A’s forecasting performance: - \(P(\text{Predict Hike}\mid H)=0.80\). - \(P(\text{Predict Hike}\mid H')=0.20\).

Tasks 1. Draw the full probability tree (first split by true outcome \(H\) vs. \(H'\), then by Analyst A’s prediction). 2. Compute the overall probability that Analyst A predicts a hike. 3. Using Bayes’ theorem, compute \(P(H\mid \text{Predict Hike})\).
