Why model several series together

Four uses of time-series econometrics

  1. Data description: levels, growth, seasonality, volatility, co-movement
  2. Forecasting: predict, then evaluate against outcomes
  3. Structural inference: how shocks propagate, and through which channels
  4. Policy analysis: counterfactual responses to policy or technology shocks

1–2 need only a reduced form; 3–4 need a structural interpretation. The gap between the two is this lecture’s theme.

From one equation to a system

Other variables none exogenous endogenous, with feedback
Equations 1 1 \(k\)

Typical use forecasting effect of a known input shock transmission

In a VAR every variable is regressed on \(p\) lags of itself and all others; none is declared exogenous in advance.

Sims (1980): the restrictions identifying large macro models were “incredible”. Let the data describe the dynamics before imposing structure.

From the structural VAR to the reduced form

A bivariate structural VAR

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_t &= b_{10} - b_{12}Z_t + c_{11}Y_{t-1} + c_{12}Z_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{yt},\\ Z_t &= b_{20} - b_{21}Y_t + c_{21}Y_{t-1} + c_{22}Z_{t-1} + \varepsilon_{zt} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(Y_t,Z_t\sim I(0)\); \(\varepsilon_{yt},\varepsilon_{zt}\) uncorrelated white noise with variances \(\sigma_y^2,\sigma_z^2\)
  • Order \(p=1\), dimension \(k=2\)
  • \(-b_{12}\), \(-b_{21}\): contemporaneous effects of each variable on the other

The \(\varepsilon\)’s are structural shocks: uncorrelated, with an economic label (demand, policy). The \(b_{ij}\) and \(c_{ij}\) describe behaviour.

OLS fails on the structural equations

Predict: is the regressor \(Z_t\) correlated with the error \(\varepsilon_{yt}\)?

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(Z_t,\varepsilon_{yt})=\frac{-b_{21}\,\sigma_y^2}{1-b_{12}b_{21}}\neq0\qquad\text{whenever }b_{21}\neq0 \]

The simultaneous-equations problem: OLS on the structural equations is inconsistent.

Structural and reduced form

In matrix form, the structural VAR (SVAR) is \[ BX_t=\Gamma_0+\Gamma_1X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}1&b_{12}\\b_{21}&1\end{bmatrix},\quad X_t=\begin{bmatrix}Y_t\\Z_t\end{bmatrix}. \]

If \(B\) is nonsingular, premultiplying by \(B^{-1}\) gives the reduced (standard) form: \[ X_t=A_0+A_1X_{t-1}+e_t,\qquad A_i=B^{-1}\Gamma_i,\quad e_t=B^{-1}\varepsilon_t . \]

With \(p\) lags: \(X_t=A_0+A_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+A_pX_{t-p}+e_t\).

What the reduced form buys, and what it hides

  • OLS works. The regressors are predetermined, so OLS equation by equation is consistent, and efficient too (same regressors, so SUR = OLS).

  • The structure is absorbed. \(A_0\), \(A_1\) and \(e_t\) mix the structural parameters.

  • The errors are correlated mixtures of both shocks:

    \[ e_{1t}=\frac{\varepsilon_{yt}-b_{12}\varepsilon_{zt}}{1-b_{12}b_{21}},\qquad e_{2t}=\frac{\varepsilon_{zt}-b_{21}\varepsilon_{yt}}{1-b_{12}b_{21}},\qquad \Sigma_e=B^{-1}\Sigma_\varepsilon B^{-1\prime}. \]

The identification problem: \(\Sigma_e\) has 3 distinct elements but depends on 4 unknowns: \(b_{12}\), \(b_{21}\), \(\sigma_y^2\), \(\sigma_z^2\).

Stability and the VMA representation

Stability of a VAR(1)

In deviations from the mean, \(X_t=A_1X_{t-1}+e_t\), so \[ X_t=A_1^{\,t}X_0+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}A_1^{\,j}e_{t-j}. \]

Stable (\(A_1^{\,j}\to0\)) \(\iff\) every eigenvalue of \(A_1\) has \(|\lambda|<1\) \(\iff\) the roots \(z_i=1/\lambda_i\) of \[ \det(I-A_1z)=(1-\lambda_1z)(1-\lambda_2z) \] lie outside the unit circle. A stable VAR is covariance stationary.

The eigenvalues are the inverse roots, as for the AR(2) in Lecture 10. A diagonal \(A_1\) gives two unrelated AR(1)s.

Each variable is a univariate ARMA(2,1)

Multiplying \((I-A_1L)X_t=e_t\) by the adjugate of \(I-A_1L\): \[ \det(I-A_1L)\,Y_t=(1-a_{22}L)\,e_{1t}+a_{12}\,e_{2,t-1}. \]

\(Y_t\) and \(Z_t\) are both ARMA(2,1) with the same AR polynomial. A series that looks like a univariate ARMA may be one element of a small VAR.

VAR(\(p\)): the companion form

Stacking \(p\) lags gives a VAR(1) in the \(kp\)-vector \(\mathbf X_t=(X_t',\dots,X_{t-p+1}')'\): \[ \mathbf X_t=\Pi_0+\Pi_1\mathbf X_{t-1}+E_t,\qquad \Pi_1=\begin{bmatrix} A_1&A_2&\cdots&A_{p-1}&A_p\\ I&0&\cdots&0&0\\ 0&I&\cdots&0&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\ 0&0&\cdots&I&0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Stable \(\iff\) all \(kp\) eigenvalues of \(\Pi_1\) have modulus \(<1\) \(\iff\) all roots of \(\det(I-A_1z-\cdots-A_pz^p)\) lie outside the unit circle.

Unit root: \(z=1\) is a root iff \(\det A(1)=0\), which means a stochastic trend. Whether some combination of the variables is stationary is the question of cointegration.

Two first-order systems

\(A_1\) Eigenvalues Roots \(z\) Half-life \(\ln0.5/\ln\lambda_{\max}\)

Both are stable. The second returns as the worked example.

A root just outside the unit circle means stable, but slow.

The VMA (Wold) representation

If the VAR is stable, \(A(L)=I-A_1L-\cdots-A_pL^p\) is invertible and \[ X_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Phi_je_{t-j},\qquad \mu=A(1)^{-1}A_0,\qquad \Phi_0=I,\quad \Phi_j=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(j,p)}A_i\Phi_{j-i}. \] For a VAR(1), \(\Phi_j=A_1^{\,j}\).

  • Variance: \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\sum_j\Phi_j\Sigma_e\Phi_j'\)
  • \(n\)-step forecast error: \(X_{t+n}-E_tX_{t+n}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\Phi_je_{t+n-j}\)

Every stationary VAR is a VMA(\(\infty\)) in its one-step forecast errors: the multivariate Wold representation. The \(\Phi_j\) are the building blocks of impulse responses and variance decompositions.

Impulse responses and identification

Forecast-error impulse responses

The elements of \(\Phi_h\), \[ \phi_{ij,h}=\frac{\partial X_{i,t+h}}{\partial e_{jt}}, \] give the response of variable \(i\), \(h\) periods later, to a unit reduced-form innovation \(e_{jt}\).

They have two problems:

  1. \(\Phi_0=I\) moves \(e_1\) while holding \(e_2\) fixed. Since \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) are correlated, that experiment is unrepresentative.
  2. Each \(e_{jt}\) mixes the structural shocks, so it has no economic label.

Structural impulse responses

Substituting \(e_t=B^{-1}\varepsilon_t\): \[ X_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\qquad \Psi_j=\Phi_jB^{-1},\qquad \psi_{ij,h}=\frac{\partial X_{i,t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_{jt}} . \]

  • Impact: \(\Psi_0=B^{-1}\); for a VAR(1), \(\Psi_h=A_1\Psi_{h-1}\)
  • Decay: \(\Psi_h\to0\) if the VAR is stable
  • Long-run cumulated effect: \(\sum_h\Psi_h=A(1)^{-1}B^{-1}\). For growth rates, this is the effect on the level.
  • Scaling: one-s.d. shocks use \(\Psi_h\Sigma_\varepsilon^{1/2}\), which changes magnitudes but not shapes

Identification: counting parameters

Can we recover the structure from \(\hat A_i\) and \(\hat\Sigma_e\)? Not without restrictions (Sims 1980).

Shock (co)variances 3 2

Given \(B\), everything else follows (\(\Gamma_i=BA_i\)), so the shortfall lies in \(\Sigma_e=B^{-1}\Sigma_\varepsilon B^{-1\prime}\). In general there are \(k(k+1)/2\) moments for \(k^2\) unknowns, so we need \[ \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\ \text{restrictions}\qquad(1,\ 3,\ 10\ \text{for}\ k=2,\ 3,\ 5). \]

Recursive identification

Recursive ordering (Sims 1980): earlier variables can affect later ones within the period, but not vice versa.

Assume \(Z_t\) does not respond to \(Y_t\) within the period. Then \(b_{21}=0\) and \(Z\) is ordered first: \[ B=\begin{bmatrix}1&b_{12}\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad e_{1t}=\varepsilon_{yt}-b_{12}\varepsilon_{zt},\qquad e_{2t}=\varepsilon_{zt}. \]

This gives three equations in three unknowns: \[ \sigma_1^2=\sigma_y^2+b_{12}^2\sigma_z^2,\quad \sigma_2^2=\sigma_z^2,\quad \sigma_{12}=-b_{12}\sigma_z^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_{12}=-\frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_2^2},\quad \sigma_y^2=\sigma_1^2-\frac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_2^2}. \]

Exactly identified. With \(k\) variables, a recursive ordering zeroes one triangle of \(B\), which gives exactly \(k(k-1)/2\) restrictions.

The Cholesky factorization does it in one step

Let \(P\equiv B^{-1}\Sigma_\varepsilon^{1/2}\). Then \(\Sigma_e=PP'\), and under a recursive ordering \(P\) is triangular: it is the unique Cholesky factor of \(\Sigma_e\). In the \(2\times2\) case: \[ \begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{12}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}=PP',\qquad P=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{a_{11}}&0\\[2pt]\dfrac{a_{12}}{\sqrt{a_{11}}}&\sqrt{a_{22}-\dfrac{a_{12}^2}{a_{11}}}\end{bmatrix}. \]

  • \(\operatorname{diag}(P)\) gives the structural standard deviations
  • dividing each column of \(P\) by its diagonal element gives \(B^{-1}\)
  • \(\Phi_hP\) gives the orthogonalized (one-s.d.) impulse responses

The ordering is an assumption

With \(Y\) first (\(b_{12}=0\)), the \(2\times2\) formula gives \[ \sigma_y^2=\sigma_1^2,\qquad b_{21}=-\frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1^2},\qquad \sigma_z^2=\sigma_2^2-\frac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_1^2}. \] To put \(Z\) first instead, reorder \(\Sigma_e\) as \((Z,Y)\), factor it, and reorder back.

  • The first variable responds on impact only to its own shock, and its shock moves every variable.
  • The last variable responds on impact to all shocks.

Different orderings give different IRFs and FEVDs, and the gap grows with the residual correlations. Let economic judgment choose: which variable cannot react within the period?

Other identifying restrictions

  1. Non-recursive short-run zeros in \(B\), or a known elasticity (Sims 1986; Bernanke 1986)
  2. Long-run restrictions on \(\sum_h\Psi_h\): e.g. demand shocks have no long-run effect on output (Blanchard and Quah 1989)
  3. Sign restrictions on responses, which identify a set of structures (Uhlig 2005)
  4. External instruments correlated with only one shock (Stock and Watson 2018)

Once \(B\) is pinned down: \(\hat\varepsilon_t=\hat B\hat e_t\) and \(\hat\Psi_h=\hat\Phi_h\hat B^{-1}\).

Variance decomposition

Forecast-error variance decomposition

The \(n\)-step forecast error in terms of structural shocks is \[ X_{t+n}-E_tX_{t+n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Psi_i\varepsilon_{t+n-i}. \] The shocks are uncorrelated, so the variance splits into one term per shock: \[ \sigma_Y(n)^2=\sigma_y^2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\psi_{11,i}^2+\sigma_z^2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\psi_{12,i}^2, \qquad \text{share of }\varepsilon_z=\frac{\sigma_z^2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\psi_{12,i}^2}{\sigma_Y(n)^2}. \]

With Cholesky identification, each term is a sum of squared one-s.d. responses \(\Phi_iP\). IRFs and the FEVD together make up innovation accounting.

Reading a variance decomposition

  • If \(\varepsilon_z\) explains none of \(Y\)’s forecast-error variance, \(Y\) is exogenous to \(Z\). If it explains most of it, \(Y\) is highly endogenous.
  • A variable typically explains most of its own variance at short horizons and less at long ones.

Under a recursive ordering, the first variable’s one-step variance comes entirely from its own shock by construction. That is an assumption, not a finding.

Worked example: Cholesky identification step by step

Steps 1–2: reduced form and ordering

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_t &= 0.7\,Y_{t-1}+0.2\,Z_{t-1}+e_{1t},\\ Z_t &= 0.2\,Y_{t-1}+0.7\,Z_{t-1}+e_{2t}, \end{aligned} \qquad \Sigma_e=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0.4\\0.4&0.5\end{bmatrix} \]

  • Stable: the eigenvalues are \(0.9\) and \(0.5\)
  • Residual correlation \(0.4/0.5=0.8\): high, so the ordering will matter
  • Ordering: assume \(Z_t\) does not respond to \(Y_t\) within the period (\(b_{21}=0\), \(Z\) first). That leaves 3 unknowns and 3 moments, so the model is exactly identified.

Steps 3–4: Cholesky factor and structure

Factor \(\Sigma_e\) in the order \((Z,Y)\), then return to the order \((Y,Z)\): \[ P_{(Z,Y)}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{0.5}&0\\[2pt]0.4/\sqrt{0.5}&\sqrt{0.5-0.4^2/0.5}\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}0.7071&0\\0.5657&0.4243\end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P=\begin{bmatrix}0.4243&0.5657\\0&0.7071\end{bmatrix}. \]

Match with \(P=B^{-1}\Sigma_\varepsilon^{1/2}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_y&-b_{12}\sigma_z\\0&\sigma_z\end{bmatrix}\): \[ \sigma_z^2=0.5,\qquad \sigma_y^2=0.18,\qquad -b_{12}=0.5657/0.7071=0.8 . \]

Step 5: structural coefficients and shocks

With \(\Gamma_1=BA_1\): \(c_{11}=0.7-0.8(0.2)=0.54\) and \(c_{12}=0.2-0.8(0.7)=-0.36\), so \[ \begin{aligned} Y_t &= 0.8\,Z_t+0.54\,Y_{t-1}-0.36\,Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{yt}, &\quad \sigma_y^2&=0.18,\\ Z_t &= 0.2\,Y_{t-1}+0.7\,Z_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{zt}, &\quad \sigma_z^2&=0.5, \end{aligned} \] with \(e_{1t}=\varepsilon_{yt}+0.8\,\varepsilon_{zt}\) and \(e_{2t}=\varepsilon_{zt}\).

\(c_{12}=-0.36\) but \(a_{12}=0.2\): reduced-form coefficients can differ in sign from the structural ones.

Step 6: structural impulse responses

\(\Psi_0=B^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.8\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\), and then \(\Psi_h=A_1\Psi_{h-1}\):

\(h\) \(Y\) to \(\varepsilon_y\) \(Z\) to \(\varepsilon_y\) \(Y\) to \(\varepsilon_z\) \(Z\) to \(\varepsilon_z\)
0 1.00 0.00 0.80 1.00
1 0.70 0.20 0.76 0.86
2 0.53 0.28 0.70 0.75
  • \(Z\) does not respond to \(\varepsilon_y\) on impact, by assumption.
  • \(\varepsilon_z\) has the larger effect on \(Y\): it also moves \(Z\), which feeds back into \(Y\).
  • The responses decay at about the rate of the dominant eigenvalue, \(0.9\). For one-s.d. shocks, scale by \(\sigma_y=0.42\) and \(\sigma_z=0.71\).

Step 7: variance decomposition at \(n=1\)

The one-step forecast error is \(\Psi_0\varepsilon_{t+1}\). Using the one-s.d. responses \(P\):

  • \(Y\): \(0.4243^2+0.5657^2=0.18+0.32=0.5\), so 36% comes from \(\varepsilon_y\) and 64% from \(\varepsilon_z\)
  • \(Z\): \(0.7071^2=0.5\), so 100% comes from \(\varepsilon_z\), as the recursive restriction imposes

Predict: as the horizon \(n\) grows, does \(\varepsilon_z\)’s share of \(Y\)’s variance rise or fall?

Step 8: reverse the ordering

With \(Y\) first (\(b_{12}=0\)): \[ P=\begin{bmatrix}0.7071&0\\0.5657&0.4243\end{bmatrix},\qquad \sigma_y^2=0.5,\quad \sigma_z^2=0.18,\quad b_{21}=-0.8 . \]

One-step share of \(\varepsilon_z\) in… \(Z\) first \(Y\) first
\(Y\)’s variance 64% 0%
\(Z\)’s variance 100% 36%

Every \((b_{12},b_{21})\) on the curve \(b_{21}=-(\sigma_{12}+b_{12}\sigma_2^2)/(\sigma_1^2+b_{12}\sigma_{12})\) fits \(\Sigma_e\) equally well. The two orderings are its intercepts, \((-0.8,0)\) and \((0,-0.8)\).

With \(\rho=0.8\) the ordering decides the answer. Check the residual correlations, justify the ordering, and report how the results change under other orderings.

The worked example with residual correlation \(\rho\). The lines show \(\varepsilon_z\)’s share of \(Y\)’s \(n\)-step forecast-error variance under each ordering.

fev = {
  const s = 0.5, s12 = rho * s;
  const A = [[0.7, 0.2], [0.2, 0.7]];
  const mul = (X, Y) => [
    [X[0][0] * Y[0][0] + X[0][1] * Y[1][0], X[0][0] * Y[0][1] + X[0][1] * Y[1][1]],
    [X[1][0] * Y[0][0] + X[1][1] * Y[1][0], X[1][0] * Y[0][1] + X[1][1] * Y[1][1]]
  ];
  // Cholesky factors in the order (Y, Z); columns = (eps_y, eps_z)
  const Pz = [[Math.sqrt(s - s12 * s12 / s), s12 / Math.sqrt(s)], [0, Math.sqrt(s)]];  // Z first
  const Py = [[Math.sqrt(s), 0], [s12 / Math.sqrt(s), Math.sqrt(s - s12 * s12 / s)]];  // Y first
  const out = [];
  let F = [[1, 0], [0, 1]];          // Phi_h = A1^h
  let cz = [0, 0], cy = [0, 0];      // cumulated squared responses of Y to (eps_y, eps_z)
  for (let n = 1; n <= 20; n++) {
    const Rz = mul(F, Pz), Ry = mul(F, Py);
    cz = [cz[0] + Rz[0][0] ** 2, cz[1] + Rz[0][1] ** 2];
    cy = [cy[0] + Ry[0][0] ** 2, cy[1] + Ry[0][1] ** 2];
    out.push({n: n, ordering: "Z first", share: 100 * cz[1] / (cz[0] + cz[1])});
    out.push({n: n, ordering: "Y first", share: 100 * cy[1] / (cy[0] + cy[1])});
    F = mul(A, F);
  }
  return out;
}

1. Reduced form OLS \(\hat A_1\), \(\hat\Sigma_e\); stability, residual correlation eigenvalues 0.9, 0.5; \(\rho_{12}=0.8\)
2. Ordering which variable cannot respond within the period? \(Z\) first (\(b_{21}=0\))
3. Cholesky factor factor \(\hat\Sigma_e\) in the chosen order, reorder back \(P=\begin{bmatrix}0.4243&0.5657\\0&0.7071\end{bmatrix}\)
4. Structure \(\sigma\)’s from \(\operatorname{diag}(P)\); \(B^{-1}\) = columns of \(P\) ÷ diagonal \(\sigma_y^2=0.18\), \(\sigma_z^2=0.5\), \(b_{12}=-0.8\)
5. Coefficients and shocks \(\Gamma_i=BA_i\); \(\varepsilon_t=Be_t\) \(c_{11}=0.54\), \(c_{12}=-0.36\)
6. Impulse responses \(\Phi_hB^{-1}\) (one unit) or \(\Phi_hP\) (one s.d.) impact \((1,0)\) and \((0.8,1)\)
7. Variance decomposition sums of squared one-s.d. responses \(Y\): 64% \(\varepsilon_z\) at \(n=1\)
8. Robustness repeat with other plausible orderings conclusions reverse

Specification, estimation and diagnostics

Choosing the lag length

LR test (Sims 1980), with both models estimated on the same sample: \[ LR=(T-c)\big(\ln|\hat\Sigma_r|-\ln|\hat\Sigma_u|\big)\ \overset{a}{\sim}\ \chi^2\big(k^2(p_u-p_r)\big), \] where \(c=1+kp_u\) is the number of parameters per unrestricted equation (a small-sample correction).

Information criteria, with \(N=k^2p+k\) parameters in total: \[ AIC=T\ln|\hat\Sigma|+2N,\qquad SBC=T\ln|\hat\Sigma|+N\ln T . \]

Minimize on a common sample. SBC picks shorter lags than AIC. Adding lags does not fix omitted variables.

Estimation and diagnostics

  • Estimate by OLS equation by equation, choosing the variables by theory.
  • Over-parameterized (\(k+pk^2\) coefficients): interpret the IRFs and FEVDs, not single coefficients.
  • Residuals: test for serial correlation with multivariate portmanteau or LM tests; check normality and heteroskedasticity.
  • Stability: all companion eigenvalues must lie inside the unit circle.
  • Forecast by iterating \(E_tX_{t+n}=\hat A_0+\hat A_1E_tX_{t+n-1}+\cdots+\hat A_pE_tX_{t+n-p}\).

Recap

Key takeaways

  • Structural vs reduced form: OLS can’t estimate the SVAR but can estimate the reduced form, whose errors \(e_t=B^{-1}\varepsilon_t\) are correlated mixtures.
  • Stability: companion eigenvalues inside the unit circle ⇒ \(X_t=\mu+\sum_j\Phi_je_{t-j}\).
  • Structural IRFs: \(\Psi_h=\Phi_hB^{-1}\), with long-run effect \(A(1)^{-1}B^{-1}\).
  • Identification: we need \(k(k-1)/2\) restrictions. A recursive ordering supplies them, solved by the Cholesky factor \(P=B^{-1}\Sigma_\varepsilon^{1/2}\).
  • FEVD: sums of squared one-s.d. responses.
  • The ordering is an assumption: with \(\rho=0.8\), \(\varepsilon_z\)’s one-step share of \(Y\) swings from 64% to 0%.
  • Specification: LR or AIC/SBC on a common sample; check residuals and stability.
