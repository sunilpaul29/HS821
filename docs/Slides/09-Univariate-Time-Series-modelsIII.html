From data to model

Known parameters → estimated parameters

Lectures 6–8 treated the ARMA parameters as known: every ACF, PACF and impulse response was a population object. In practice, we observe one realization. Box-Jenkins methodology outlines the process of modelling using observed data.

The Box–Jenkins cycle

  1. Identification — use the sample ACF/PACF to shortlist candidate orders \((p,q)\).
  2. Estimation — fit each candidate (moments, least squares, ML, Bayesian); compare with information criteria.
  3. Diagnostic checking — do the residuals look like white noise? If not, back to step 1.
  4. Forecasting and evaluation — forecast with the accepted model; judge it out of sample.

All series here are stationary. Unit roots, differencing and ARIMA models come in a later lecture.

The R toolkit

Everything uses base-R stats:

Function Job
acf(), pacf() sample correlograms

R convention. arima() reports the mean \(\mu\) under the label intercept, not the constant \(c\); recover \(c=\mu(1-\phi_1-\cdots-\phi_p)\). Its MA polynomial is \(1+\beta_1L+\cdots+\beta_qL^q\) — the same sign convention as ours.

Identification: the sample correlogram

Sample moments

The population moments are estimated by their sample analogues:

\[ \bar y=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_t, \qquad \hat\gamma_j=\frac1T\sum_{t=j+1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-j}-\bar y), \qquad \hat\rho_j=\frac{\hat\gamma_j}{\hat\gamma_0}. \]

  • The divisor is \(T\), not \(T-j\). This shrinks \(\hat\gamma_j\) slightly towards zero at long lags, but guarantees that \(\hat\Gamma_p=[\hat\gamma_{|i-j|}]\) is positive definite — the reason Yule–Walker always delivers a stationary AR.
  • Long lags rest on few pairs of observations: inspect lags up to about \(T/4\).

The sample PACF \(\hat\phi_{jj}\) is the last coefficient in the regression of \(y_t\) on \(y_{t-1},\dots,y_{t-j}\) — equivalently, it comes from the \(\hat\rho_j\) by the Durbin–Levinson recursion.

How large is “large”? Sampling distributions

White noise. If \(Y_t\) is i.i.d. with finite variance, for each \(j\ge1\) \[ \hat\rho_j\;\overset{a}{\sim}\;N\!\left(0,\tfrac1T\right), \] asymptotically independent across lags \(\Rightarrow\) 95% bands \(\pm2/\sqrt T\).

MA(\(q\)): Bartlett’s formula. Beyond the cut-off, \[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat\rho_j)\approx\frac1T\Big(1+2\sum_{i=1}^{q}\rho_i^2\Big),\qquad j>q . \] The bands widen with \(q\) — a sequential test of the MA order (acf(x, ci.type = "ma")).

AR(\(p\)): Quenouille’s result. \[ \hat\phi_{jj}\;\overset{a}{\sim}\;N\!\left(0,\tfrac1T\right),\qquad j>p, \] so \(\pm2/\sqrt T\) bands on the sample PACF test the AR order directly.

Two cautions

  1. Multiple testing. With 20 lags and 5% bands, about one spike will cross the bands by chance even for white noise.
  2. Correlation across lags. Sample autocorrelations of a dependent series are correlated with one another, so a sample ACF looks smoother, with longer waves, than its population counterpart.

Do not chase isolated spikes at long, uninterpretable lags.

Reading the graphs

  • White noise: none of the 20 sample autocorrelations crosses the bands in this draw (lags 12 and 14 come close). One crossing in twenty would have been entirely normal.
  • MA(1) ACF: one large spike at lag 1 — 0.32, well below the population value 0.47, a reminder of sampling noise at \(T=100\) — then inside the bands, apart from an isolated spike at lag 20 that just reaches Bartlett’s band: exactly the kind of long lag not to chase.

Portmanteau tests: Box–Pierce and Ljung–Box

Test the first \(K\) autocorrelations jointly, \(H_0:\rho_1=\cdots=\rho_K=0\):

\[ Q_{BP}(K)=T\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat\rho_k^{\,2}, \qquad Q_{LB}(K)=T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\hat\rho_k^{\,2}}{T-k}. \]

Both \(\overset{a}{\sim}\chi^2(K)\) under \(H_0\). The Ljung–Box weights \((T+2)/(T-k)>1\) correct Box–Pierce, whose \(\chi^2\) approximation is poor in small samples.

On residuals the degrees of freedom change. For the residuals of an estimated ARMA(\(p,q\)), the \(p+q\) coefficients absorb part of the autocorrelation: \[ Q(K)\;\overset{a}{\sim}\;\chi^2(K-p-q) \qquad(\texttt{Box.test(e, lag = K, type = "Ljung-Box", fitdf = p + q)}). \]

Choosing \(K\). Small \(K\) lacks power at longer lags; large \(K\) dilutes the test. Common choices: \(K\approx10\) (non-seasonal), \(K\approx2s\) (seasonal, period \(s\)). Reporting several \(K\) is good practice.

  c(BoxPierce = Box.test(e, lag = 10, type = "Box-Pierce")$p.value < 0.05,

# Power: the same test applied to an AR(1) with phi = 0.5

Reading the output

  • Ljung–Box rejects about 5% of the time under the null (0.052), as it should.
  • Box–Pierce rejects only about half as often (0.026) — it is undersized at \(T=50\).
  • Against a genuinely dependent AR(1) series, the Ljung–Box statistic is enormous (\(Q=53.7\) on 10 df, \(p\approx5\times10^{-8}\)).

This is why software and practice default to Ljung–Box.

An identification checklist

  1. Plot the series. Look for trends, breaks, outliers, changing variance and seasonality.
  2. Transform if necessary. Logs or square roots stabilize a variance that grows with the level; differencing removes stochastic trends (later lecture).
  3. Compute the sample ACF and PACF and compare with the fingerprint table of Lectures 6–8.
  4. Shortlist two to four candidates, favouring small models (parsimony).
  5. Carry all candidates into estimation; let information criteria, diagnostics and out-of-sample performance decide.

Mixed ARMA models are hard to separate by eye: both the ACF and the PACF decay.

Estimation I: the method of moments

The estimation problem

For an ARMA(\(p,q\)) we must estimate \(\boldsymbol\theta=(c,\;\phi_1,\dots,\phi_p,\;\beta_1,\dots,\beta_q,\;\sigma^2)'\). Three features make this harder than cross-section regression:

  • Dependence. The joint density of the sample is not the product of the marginals.
  • Lagged regressors are not strictly exogenous. \(Y_{t-1}\) is uncorrelated with \(\varepsilon_t\) but correlated with \(\varepsilon_{t-1},\varepsilon_{t-2},\dots\) — this biases least squares in finite samples.
  • MA terms are unobserved. Lagged shocks must be reconstructed from the data, making estimation nonlinear.
Approach Idea Natural for
Method of moments (Yule–Walker) match population and sample autocovariances pure AR
Least squares (OLS, CSS) minimize squared one-step errors, conditioning on initial values AR (OLS); ARMA (CSS)
Exact maximum likelihood maximize the full Gaussian joint density any ARMA
Bayesian likelihood × prior → posterior any ARMA; small samples

The Yule–Walker equations

Multiply \(X_t=\phi_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pX_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t\) by \(X_{t-j}\), \(j=1,\dots,p\), and take expectations:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \gamma_0 & \gamma_1 & \cdots & \gamma_{p-1}\\ \gamma_1 & \gamma_0 & \cdots & \gamma_{p-2}\\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots\\ \gamma_{p-1} & \gamma_{p-2} & \cdots & \gamma_0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\Gamma_p} \begin{bmatrix}\phi_1\\ \vdots\\ \phi_p\end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_1\\ \vdots\\ \gamma_p\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\gamma_p}, \qquad \sigma^2=\gamma_0-\boldsymbol\phi'\boldsymbol\gamma_p . \]

Replacing population by sample moments gives the Yule–Walker estimator

\[ \boxed{\;\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}_{YW}=\hat\Gamma_p^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_p, \qquad \hat\sigma^2=\hat\gamma_0-\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}'\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_p, \qquad \hat c=\bar y\,(1-\hat\phi_1-\cdots-\hat\phi_p).\;} \]

For an AR(1), \(\hat\phi=\hat\rho_1\). For an AR(2): \[ \hat\phi_1=\frac{\hat\rho_1(1-\hat\rho_2)}{1-\hat\rho_1^2}, \qquad \hat\phi_2=\frac{\hat\rho_2-\hat\rho_1^2}{1-\hat\rho_1^2}. \]

Properties of Yule–Walker

  • Closed form, no iteration.
  • \(\hat\Gamma_p\) positive definite \(\Rightarrow\) \(\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}_{YW}\) is always stationary.
  • \(\sqrt T(\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}_{YW}-\boldsymbol\phi)\overset{d}{\to}N(\mathbf 0,\;\sigma^2\Gamma_p^{-1})\) — the same as maximum likelihood, so YW is asymptotically efficient for pure AR. For an AR(1), \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\phi)\approx(1-\phi^2)/T\).
  • The last coefficient \(\hat\phi_{pp}\) is the sample PACF at lag \(p\).

In small samples YW is biased towards zero, most severely when roots are close to the unit circle.

Predict: we simulate an AR(2) with \((\phi_1,\phi_2)=(0.5,0.25)\), \(T=250\). Will the matrix form, the AR(2) formulas, ar.yw() and the lag-2 sample PACF agree — and on what?

R — Yule–Walker by hand

set.seed(2024)
y_ar2 <- arima.sim(model = list(ar = c(0.5, 0.25)), n = 250)

g      <- acf(y_ar2, type = "covariance", plot = FALSE, lag.max = 2)$acf[, 1, 1]  # gamma_0..gamma_2
Gam    <- matrix(c(g[1], g[2], g[2], g[1]), 2, 2)
phi_yw <- solve(Gam, g[2:3])
s2_yw  <- g[1] - sum(phi_yw * g[2:3])
r      <- g / g[1]

cat("True (phi1, phi2)            : 0.5000 0.2500\n")
cat("Yule-Walker, matrix form     :", round(phi_yw, 4), "  sigma2 =", round(s2_yw, 4), "\n")
cat("Yule-Walker, AR(2) formulas  :", round(c(r[2] * (1 - r[3]) / (1 - r[2]^2),
                                            (r[3] - r[2]^2) / (1 - r[2]^2)), 4), "\n")
cat("ar.yw()                      :", round(ar.yw(y_ar2, order.max = 2, aic = FALSE)$ar, 4), "\n")
cat("Sample PACF at lag 2         :", round(pacf(y_ar2, plot = FALSE)$acf[2], 4), "  (= phi_22)\n")
cat("Asymptotic s.e.              :", round(sqrt(diag(s2_yw * solve(Gam) / length(y_ar2))), 4), "\n")

What you should see

  • All three routes give identical estimates, \((\hat\phi_1,\hat\phi_2)=(0.522,\,0.172)\): the matrix form, the closed-form AR(2) formulas and ar.yw() are the same calculation.
  • The lag-2 sample PACF equals \(\hat\phi_{2}\) exactly (0.1716).
  • \(\hat\phi_2=0.17\) is about 1.3 standard errors (s.e. \(\approx0.062\)) below the true 0.25 — sampling noise, consistent with the downward small-sample pull.

Yule–Walker and the PACF are the same calculation. Identifying an AR order with the sample PACF and estimating it by Yule–Walker use exactly the same numbers.

MA models resist the method of moments

For an MA(1), \(\rho_1=\beta/(1+\beta^2)\). Matching moments gives \(\hat\rho_1\beta^2-\beta+\hat\rho_1=0\), whose invertible root is

\[ \hat\beta_{MM}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4\hat\rho_1^{\,2}}}{2\hat\rho_1}. \]

A real solution exists only if \(|\hat\rho_1|\le0.5\), because \(|\rho_1|\le0.5\) for every MA(1). When \(\beta\) is large, sampling noise often pushes \(|\hat\rho_1|\) above 0.5 — and the estimator does not exist. Even when it exists it is inefficient: moment estimators are mainly useful as starting values for likelihood-based estimation.

Predict: for \(\beta=0.9\) (so \(\rho_1=0.497\)) and \(T=100\), in what share of samples does \(\hat\beta_{MM}\) fail to exist?

})

            100 * mean(abs(rho1_hat) > 0.5)))

  geom_histogram(bins = 40, fill = "grey70", colour = "white") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 0.5, linetype = "dashed", colour = "red") +

Interpretation

  • In about a third of the samples (36.4%) the moment estimator does not exist at all — even though the true model is a perfectly well-behaved, invertible MA(1).
  • The true \(\rho_1=0.497\) sits right at the boundary, so the sampling distribution of \(\hat\rho_1\) straddles 0.5.

Likelihood-based estimators have no such problem: they use the whole autocorrelation structure, not a single moment.

\[\boxed{\text{well-behaved model}\not\Rightarrow\text{well-behaved moment estimator}}\]

Estimation II: least squares

OLS for AR(\(p\))

Conditioning on the first \(p\) observations, an AR(\(p\)) is a linear regression for \(t=p+1,\dots,T\):

\[ Y_t=\mathbf x_t'\mathbf b+\varepsilon_t, \qquad \mathbf x_t=(1,\;Y_{t-1},\dots,Y_{t-p})', \qquad \mathbf b=(c,\;\phi_1,\dots,\phi_p)', \]

\[ \hat{\mathbf b}_{OLS}=\Big(\sum_{t=p+1}^{T}\mathbf x_t\mathbf x_t'\Big)^{-1}\sum_{t=p+1}^{T}\mathbf x_tY_t, \qquad \hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{T-p}\sum_{t=p+1}^{T}\hat\varepsilon_t^{\,2}. \]

\(E(\varepsilon_t\mid\mathbf x_t)=0\) (contemporaneous exogeneity) \(\Rightarrow\) OLS is consistent and asymptotically normal; the usual OLS standard errors are valid in large samples.

…but biased in finite samples

The regressors are not strictly exogenous: \(\varepsilon_t\) feeds into the future regressors \(Y_t,Y_{t+1},\dots\), so \(E(\hat{\mathbf b})\ne\mathbf b\) in finite samples. For an AR(1) with an estimated intercept (Kendall 1954; Marriott and Pope 1954):

\[ E(\hat\phi_{OLS})-\phi\;\approx\;-\frac{1+3\phi}{T}. \]

The bias is downward and grows with persistence: OLS understates persistence, so estimated half-lives are too short.

Predict: we draw 2000 samples from an AR(1) with \(\phi=0.9\) at \(T=25,50,100,250\) and estimate \(\phi\) by OLS. What sign does the bias have? Roughly how large is it at \(T=25\)?

set.seed(123)

Conditional least squares for ARMA(\(p,q\))

With MA terms the lagged shocks are unobserved, so build them recursively. For trial values of \((c,\boldsymbol\phi,\boldsymbol\beta)\),

\[ \varepsilon_t(\boldsymbol\phi,\boldsymbol\beta)=Y_t-c-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_iY_{t-i}-\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_j\,\varepsilon_{t-j}(\boldsymbol\phi,\boldsymbol\beta), \qquad t=p+1,\dots,T, \]

with pre-sample shocks set to zero.

Conditional sum of squares (CSS) minimizes \(S(c,\boldsymbol\phi,\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_t\varepsilon_t^2\). It is nonlinear in \(\boldsymbol\beta\), so it is solved numerically: arima(y, order = c(p, 0, q), method = "CSS").

Invertibility is what makes CSS work. An error in the arbitrary start \(\varepsilon_p=0\) enters \(\varepsilon_t\) multiplied by \((-\beta)^{t-p}\) in an MA(1). It dies out only if \(|\beta|<1\). Near the boundary CSS performs poorly and exact maximum likelihood is preferred.

Recap

Key takeaways

  • Box–Jenkins: identify → estimate → check → forecast, and loop back when the residuals say so.
  • Sampling bands: \(\pm2/\sqrt T\) under white noise; Bartlett widens ACF bands beyond an MA(\(q\)) cut-off; Quenouille justifies \(\pm2/\sqrt T\) on the PACF beyond an AR(\(p\)).
  • Don’t chase isolated spikes at long lags — about one in twenty crosses by chance.
  • Ljung–Box has the right size in small samples; Box–Pierce is undersized. On residuals use \(\chi^2(K-p-q)\).
  • Yule–Walker is closed form, always stationary, asymptotically efficient for pure AR — and identical to the PACF calculation.
  • MA moment estimators can fail to exist (\(|\hat\rho_1|>0.5\)); use them only as starting values.
  • OLS for AR is consistent but biased downward by about \(-(1+3\phi)/T\): it understates persistence.
  • CSS extends least squares to ARMA, and relies on invertibility.
