From probability to inference

Recap — three coin tosses

One simple experiment connects most of the ideas from the previous lecture.

Concept In this example
Experiment Toss three coins
Sample space \(\{HHH,HHT,HTH,HTT,THH,THT,TTH,TTT\}\)
Event Exactly two Heads: \(\{HHT,HTH,THH\}\)
Random variable \(X=\) number of Heads
Probability distribution \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(3,0.5)\)

From probability to data

Suppose the three-coin experiment is repeated five times and the observed numbers of Heads are

\[ \{2,1,3,2,0\}. \]

Concept In this example
Population Possible observations generated by repeated experiments
Parameter \(p=0.5,\quad \mu=E(X)=1.5\)
Sample \(\{2,1,3,2,0\}\)
Sample statistic \(\bar X=1.6,\quad \hat p=8/15\)

The shift to inference: the probability model describes what could happen; the sample tells us what actually happened. Statistical inference uses the sample to learn about unknown population parameters.

Estimators

What is an estimator?

An estimator \(\hat\theta\) is a rule — a function of the sample data — used to estimate an unknown population parameter \(\theta\).

Because it depends on a random sample, \(\hat\theta\) is itself a random variable and therefore has a sampling distribution.

The quality of an estimator is judged by the location and spread of its sampling distribution, and by what happens as the sample size grows.

Bias and efficiency

Bias measures systematic error across repeated samples:

\[ \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)=E(\hat\theta)-\theta. \]

An estimator is unbiased if

\[ E(\hat\theta)=\theta. \]

Efficiency: among unbiased estimators, the estimator with the smaller variance is more precise and is preferred.

Bias concerns the centre of the sampling distribution; efficiency concerns its spread.

Consistency and asymptotic unbiasedness

Consistency: as the sample size grows, the estimator concentrates on the true parameter:

\[ \hat\theta \xrightarrow{p}\theta. \]

Asymptotic unbiasedness: any bias vanishes as \(n\to\infty\):

\[ \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)\to0. \]

A finite-sample estimator may be biased and still be consistent if the bias disappears as the sample becomes large.

Asymptotic normality and standard error

For many estimators,

\[ \sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V). \]

This large-sample result provides the basis for approximate confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

The standard error is the estimated standard deviation of the estimator’s sampling distribution. It measures sampling uncertainty.

Small standard error \(\Rightarrow\) estimates vary less across repeated samples \(\Rightarrow\) greater precision.

Constructing estimators

Three common methods

Popular methods for constructing estimators include least squares, method of moments, and maximum likelihood.

Method Main idea
Least squares (LS) Choose parameters that make fitted values close to observed values in squared-error terms
Method of moments (MoM) Choose parameters so model-implied moments match sample moments
Maximum likelihood (ML) Choose parameters under which the observed data are most compatible with the probability model

Same objective, different routes

Least squares

Minimise a measure of prediction error.

Method of moments

Match features of the model to features of the data.

Maximum likelihood

Choose the parameter values most compatible with the observed sample under the model.

These methods construct the estimator. Inference then asks how uncertain the resulting estimate is.

Frequentist inference

The frequentist perspective

In the frequentist framework, the parameter \(\theta\) is treated as fixed but unknown.

The sample is random, so the estimator is random before the data are observed.

\[ \text{probability model} \longrightarrow \text{estimator }\hat\theta \longrightarrow \text{sampling distribution of }\hat\theta. \]

From the sampling distribution we obtain the main tools of frequentist inference.

Standard error

The standard error measures the typical sampling variation of an estimator.

Small standard error

The estimator is relatively precise across repeated samples.

Large standard error

The estimator varies substantially across repeated samples.

Confidence intervals

A 95% confidence-interval procedure is constructed so that, under repeated sampling, approximately 95% of the intervals produced by that procedure contain the true parameter.

Once an interval has been calculated from the observed sample, the standard frequentist interpretation does not assign a 95% probability to the fixed parameter being inside that particular interval.

Hypothesis tests and p-values

A hypothesis test asks whether the observed statistic would be unusual if a null hypothesis were true.

The p-value is the probability, under the null model, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one.

A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Recap

What to carry forward

  • An estimator is a random variable because it is a function of a random sample.
  • Good estimators are assessed using bias, variance/efficiency, consistency, and asymptotic behaviour.
  • LS, MoM, and ML are alternative ways of constructing estimators.
  • Frequentist inference treats the parameter as fixed and uncertainty as arising from repeated sampling.
  • Standard errors quantify sampling uncertainty.
  • Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are built from the sampling distribution.
