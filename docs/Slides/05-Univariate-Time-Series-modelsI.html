Where we are

Earlier lectures treated observations largely as a random sample. Time-series data are different: ordering matters, observations may be dependent, and we usually observe only one realization of an underlying stochastic process.

This lecture develops the probabilistic properties of univariate time series — the foundation for ARMA modelling and forecasting later in the course. Estimation, inference, and model selection come afterwards.

From samples to time series

A stochastic process vs a realization

A stochastic process is a collection of random variables indexed by time, \[\{Y_t:t\in\mathbb Z\}.\]

Before observation each \(Y_t\) is random. Once observed over \(t=1,\ldots,T\) we obtain one realization, \[y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_T.\]

A time series is not an unordered collection of observations. Its temporal ordering and dependence structure are part of the object being studied.

Shuffle a cross-section and little is lost. Shuffle a time series and its dependence — its time-series structure — is destroyed.

Moments of a stochastic process

Mean function: \[\mu_t=E(Y_t).\]

Autocovariance (\(h\) periods apart): \[\gamma(t,t-h)=\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-h})=E[(Y_t-\mu_t)(Y_{t-h}-\mu_{t-h})].\]

For a covariance-stationary process these depend only on the lag \(h\): \[\gamma_h=\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-h}),\qquad \rho_h=\frac{\gamma_h}{\gamma_0}.\]

The sequence \(\{\rho_h\}\) is the autocorrelation function (ACF); its plot against \(h\) is the correlogram.

Restrictions on an autocovariance function

For a covariance-stationary process, \[\gamma_h=\gamma_{-h},\quad \rho_h=\rho_{-h},\quad \gamma_0=\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)\ge0,\quad \rho_0=1,\quad |\rho_h|\le1.\]

A deeper restriction: for any constants \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\) and indices \(t_1,\ldots,t_m\), \[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_i a_j\,\gamma_{t_i-t_j}\ge0.\]

The autocovariance sequence must be positive semidefinite.

Stationarity

Strict vs covariance stationarity

Strict stationarity — shifting time changes no finite-dimensional joint distribution: \[(Y_{t_1},\ldots,Y_{t_k})\overset{d}{=}(Y_{t_1+h},\ldots,Y_{t_k+h}).\]

Covariance (weak, second-order) stationarity:

  1. \(E(Y_t)=\mu\) for all \(t\);
  2. \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=\gamma_0<\infty\) for all \(t\);
  3. \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-h})=\gamma_h\) depends only on the lag \(h\).

If second moments exist, strict \(\Rightarrow\) covariance stationarity (not conversely). For a Gaussian process the mean and covariance pin down all distributions, so covariance stationarity \(\Rightarrow\) strict.

Predict before you simulate

Consider four processes:

\[Y_t=\varepsilon_t \quad(\text{white noise}), \qquad Y_t=2+0.03t+\varepsilon_t \quad(\text{trend}),\] \[Y_t=Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t \quad(\text{random walk}), \qquad Y_t=t\,\varepsilon_t \quad(\text{time-varying variance}).\]

For each, ask before running:

  • Is the mean constant?
  • Is the variance constant?
  • If not stationary, which condition fails?

Reading the graphs

  • White noise — constant mean and variance.
  • Deterministic trend — violates the constant-mean condition.
  • Random walk — \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t\sigma^2\) (with \(Y_0\) fixed): variance grows with time.
  • Time-varying variance — \(E(Y_t)=0\) but \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t^2\sigma^2\): nonstationary through the variance.

Stationarity is a property of the process, not a visual property of one observed path.

Ergodicity

Stationarity is not enough

Stationarity keeps the probability law stable over time. Ergodicity connects two very different ways of averaging.

  • Ensemble average — average across many parallel realizations (histories) at a fixed date \(t\). This is the expectation \(E(Y_t)\).
  • Time average — average along one realization over time: \(\dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_t\).

A stationary process is mean ergodic when the two coincide as the sample grows: \[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}Y_t \xrightarrow{p} E(Y_t).\]

The same idea extends to variances and autocovariances.

Why it is the load-bearing assumption

We almost never get the ensemble — we get one history. One realized path of US GDP, one path of the S&P 500, one inflation series.

Every time we estimate a mean, variance, or autocorrelation from that single path and treat it as a population quantity, we are assuming ergodicity. If it fails, more of the same history does not help.

Two warnings the simulations make concrete:

  • stationarity \(\not\Rightarrow\) ergodicity — a stable law can hide a permanent, history-specific component;
  • serial dependence \(\not\Rightarrow\) non-ergodicity — a stationary AR(1) is dependent yet ergodic.

A real-world picture: permanent vs transitory

The simulated example \(Y_t = A + \varepsilon_t\) (with \(A\) drawn once, \(\varepsilon_t\) transitory) is the permanent–transitory decomposition of applied economics.

  • \(Y_t\) = a household’s log income: permanent component \(A\) (human capital, profession) plus a good/bad year \(\varepsilon_t\) — the structure behind Friedman’s permanent-income hypothesis.
  • Or \(Y_t\) = a country’s growth: a permanent “type” \(A\) (institutions, geography) plus year-to-year shocks.

Follow one household for decades and its time-average income converges to its own \(A\), not the population mean. Watching Argentina for two centuries reveals Argentina’s \(A\), not the world-average growth rate.

Contrast a stationary AR(1): a shock’s influence decays, \(\gamma_h=\phi^h\to0\), so a long path does recover the ensemble moments.

What you should see

As \(T\) increases, \[\frac{1}{T}\sum Y_t \xrightarrow{p} 0,\qquad \frac{1}{T}\sum Y_t^2 \xrightarrow{p} 1,\qquad \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}Y_t Y_{t-1} \xrightarrow{p} 0.\]

The path never becomes smooth. What stabilizes are the time averages of functions of the process.

AR(1): the limits

\[\frac{1}{T}\sum Y_t \xrightarrow{p} 0,\qquad \frac{1}{T}\sum Y_t^2 \xrightarrow{p} 1,\qquad \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}Y_t Y_{t-1} \xrightarrow{p} \gamma_1 = 0.8.\]

Ergodicity does not require independence. Strongly serially correlated data can still reveal the stationary moments through a long enough realization.

Simulation 3 — stationary but non-ergodic

\[Y_t=A+\varepsilon_t,\qquad A\sim N(0,1),\quad \varepsilon_t\overset{iid}{\sim}N(0,1),\]

with \(A\) drawn once and held fixed. Unconditionally it is stationary: \[E(Y_t)=0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=2,\quad \gamma_h=\operatorname{Var}(A)=1\ (h>0).\]

But within one realization \(A\) never averages out. As \(T\to\infty\), \[\tfrac{1}{T}\sum Y_t \to A,\qquad \tfrac{1}{T}\sum Y_t^2 \to A^2+1,\qquad \tfrac{1}{T-1}\sum Y_t Y_{t-1} \to A^2,\]

not the ensemble values \(0,\ 2,\ 1\).

Why stationarity does not imply ergodicity

\(Y_t=A+\varepsilon_t\) is stationary, but the common component \(A\) creates dependence that never dies out: \[\gamma_h=1\qquad\text{for every }h>0.\]

By contrast, for a stationary AR(1), \[\gamma_h\propto\phi^h\to0\qquad(|\phi|<1).\]

The issue is not whether observations are dependent, but whether the dependence is persistent enough to stop time averages from revealing ensemble moments.

Many realizations make it visible

Generate many realizations of increasing length and compare their time averages.

  • Ergodic process → time averages concentrate around the ensemble mean.
  • \(Y_t=A+\varepsilon_t\) → transitory noise averages out, but \(A\) remains.

Predict the two panels before running.

Interpretation

For white noise the distribution of time averages collapses toward zero as \(T\) grows. For \(Y_t=A+\varepsilon_t\) each realization converges to its own \(A\), so the distribution does not collapse to the ensemble mean.

Ergodicity is what lets a long history substitute, in an appropriate sense, for repeated draws from the ensemble.

Why ergodicity changes decisions

A repeated gamble on wealth \(W_t\) — each period, independently, \[W_t = 1.5\,W_{t-1}\ (\text{prob }0.5),\qquad W_t = 0.6\,W_{t-1}\ (\text{prob }0.5).\]

Ensemble view (across many parallel gamblers): \[E\!\left[\tfrac{W_t}{W_{t-1}}\right]=0.5(1.5)+0.5(0.6)=1.05 \;\Rightarrow\; E[W_t]=(1.05)^t W_0.\]

Time view (one gambler’s path): \[\tfrac{1}{t}\log\tfrac{W_t}{W_0}\xrightarrow{p} 0.5\log 1.5+0.5\log 0.6 \approx -0.053.\]

Expected wealth grows ~5%/period, but the typical path shrinks ~5%/period toward zero. This is ergodicity economics (Ole Peters); it is why geometric returns, volatility drag, and the Kelly criterion matter.

R — the ensemble vs the path you live

set.seed(826)

Summary: stationarity and ergodicity

Process Stationary? Dependence? Mean ergodic? Long-run covariance
iid white noise Yes No Yes \(\gamma_h=0\) for \(h>0\)
stationary AR(1), \(|\phi|<1\) Yes Yes Yes \(\gamma_h\to0\)
\(Y_t=A+\varepsilon_t\) Yes Yes No \(\gamma_h=1\) for all \(h>0\)
random walk No Yes — variance grows with \(t\)

\[\boxed{\text{stationary}\not\Rightarrow\text{ergodic}},\qquad \boxed{\text{dependence}\not\Rightarrow\text{non-ergodic}}.\]

White noise

White noise

A white-noise process \(\{\varepsilon_t\}\) satisfies \[E(\varepsilon_t)=0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=\sigma^2,\quad \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,\varepsilon_{t-h})=0\ (h\neq0),\] so its theoretical ACF is \(\rho_0=1,\ \rho_h=0\ (h\neq0)\).

White noise means serially uncorrelated — not necessarily independent.

  • iid white noise: independent and identically distributed.
  • Gaussian white noise: a jointly Gaussian white noise; here zero autocovariance does imply independence.
  • martingale difference: \(E(\varepsilon_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=0\).

Population ACF vs sample ACF

The population ACF is a property of the process; the sample ACF is an estimate from one finite realization.

For white noise, \(\rho_h=0\ (h>0)\) — but a finite sample almost never gives sample autocorrelations that are exactly zero.

Predict: with \(T=100\) vs \(T=1000\), which sample ACF lies more tightly around zero?

The population ACF is zero at every positive lag, yet the sample ACF fluctuates around zero because it comes from one realization. As \(T\) grows the fluctuations shrink — where the distinction between process and realization becomes operational.

A stationary process and a random walk

Same shocks, different processes

Let \(X_t=+1\) or \(-1\) with probability \(0.5\) each (independent). Then \[E(X_t)=0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(X_t)=1,\quad \gamma_h=0\ (h\neq0),\] so \(\{X_t\}\) is covariance stationary.

Accumulate the same shocks: \[Y_t=Y_{t-1}+X_t,\ Y_0=0 \;\Rightarrow\; Y_t=\sum_{i=1}^{t}X_i,\quad \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t.\]

The shocks are stationary; their accumulation is not.

Differencing changes the process

For the random walk \(Y_t=Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), the level is nonstationary because \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t\sigma^2\). But \[\Delta Y_t=Y_t-Y_{t-1}=\varepsilon_t,\] which is stationary white noise.

The distinction between the properties of a level and its difference becomes central when we study unit roots and integrated processes.

Linear representations

Wold decomposition

For a covariance-stationary, purely nondeterministic process, \[Y_t-\mu=\varepsilon_t+\psi_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\psi_2\varepsilon_{t-2}+\cdots=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\] with \(\psi_0=1\) and \(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j^2<\infty\).

\(\varepsilon_t\) is the innovation — the one-step-ahead linear projection error. Innovations are serially uncorrelated by construction; they need not be iid or Gaussian.

A Wold innovation is a statistical innovation. It is not automatically a causal structural shock, and it is defined relative to linear prediction — a nonlinear forecaster may use information a linear projection cannot.

Moments of a linear process

For \(Y_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\ \varepsilon_t\sim WN(0,\sigma^2)\): \[E(Y_t)=\mu,\qquad \gamma_0=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j^2,\] \[\gamma_h=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\psi_{j+h}\qquad(h\ge0).\]

This links the dynamic response coefficients \(\{\psi_j\}\) to the dependence structure \(\{\gamma_h\}\).

Lag operator

The lag operator \(L\) shifts back one period: \[LY_t=Y_{t-1},\qquad L^jY_t=Y_{t-j}.\]

For example, \[Y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\theta_2\varepsilon_{t-2}\] becomes \[Y_t-\mu=(1+\theta_1L+\theta_2L^2)\varepsilon_t=\theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]

Lag-polynomial notation makes AR, MA, and ARMA properties far easier to express.

Familiar linear-process families

With white-noise innovations \(\varepsilon_t\):

\[\textbf{AR}(p):\quad Y_t=c+\phi_1Y_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pY_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t.\] \[\textbf{MA}(q):\quad Y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.\] \[\textbf{ARMA}(p,q):\quad Y_t=c+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_iY_{t-i}+\varepsilon_t+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}.\]

This lecture concentrates on MA processes: a finite MA is automatically stationary when the innovations are white noise with finite variance. AR and ARMA follow later.

Moving-average processes

MA(1): moments and ACF

\[Y_t=\mu+\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1},\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim WN(0,\sigma^2).\]

\[E(Y_t)=\mu,\qquad \gamma_h=\begin{cases}(1+\theta^2)\sigma^2,&h=0,\\ \theta\sigma^2,&|h|=1,\\ 0,&|h|>1.\end{cases}\]

\[\rho_1=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2},\qquad \rho_h=0\ (|h|>1).\]

  • ACF cuts off after lag 1; the sign of \(\rho_1\) is the sign of \(\theta\).
  • Since \(\left|\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2}\right|\le\frac12\), an MA(1) can never have \(|\rho_1|>0.5\).

Predict before running

Compare \[Y_t=2+\varepsilon_t+0.8\varepsilon_{t-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y_t=2+\varepsilon_t-0.8\varepsilon_{t-1}.\]

Before looking at the graphs:

  1. What sign should \(\rho_1\) have in each?
  2. What is the numerical value of \(\rho_1\)?
  3. At which lag must the theoretical ACF become exactly zero?
  4. Must the two simulated paths look obviously different?

  <div class="muted">Lag-1 autocorrelation &nbsp; ρ₁ = θ / (1 + θ²)</div>
  <div class="posterior">${rho1.toFixed(3)}</div>
  <div class="note">
    |θ| = ${Math.abs(theta).toFixed(2)} ⇒
    <b>${Math.abs(theta) < 1 ? "invertible" : (Math.abs(theta) > 1 ? "not invertible" : "unit root")}</b>
    &nbsp;·&nbsp; ACF cuts off after lag 1
    &nbsp;·&nbsp; note |ρ₁| ≤ 0.5, reached at θ = ±1
  </div>
</div>`

Move \(\theta\): the sign of \(\rho_1\) follows the sign of \(\theta\), its magnitude never exceeds \(0.5\), and \(\theta\) vs \(1/\theta\) give the same \(\rho_1\) — the invertibility ambiguity.

MA(\(q\))

\[Y_t=\mu+\sum_{j=0}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\qquad \theta_0=1,\quad \varepsilon_t\sim WN(0,\sigma^2).\]

For \(h=0,1,\ldots,q\), \[\gamma_h=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_j\theta_{j+h},\qquad \gamma_h=0\ (h>q),\] so the theoretical ACF cuts off after lag \(q\).

The cutoff is specific to linear dependence through the innovations: once \(h>q\), \(Y_t\) and \(Y_{t-h}\) share no common innovations.

MA(2): work from the common shocks

\[Y_{1t}=2+\varepsilon_t+1.0\,\varepsilon_{t-1}+0.7\,\varepsilon_{t-2},\qquad Y_{2t}=2+\varepsilon_t-1.2\,\varepsilon_{t-1}+0.25\,\varepsilon_{t-2}.\]

Before running, use \(\gamma_h=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_j\theta_{j+h}\) to answer:

  1. At which lag must each ACF become zero?
  2. For \(Y_{1t}\), what signs do \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\) take?
  3. For \(Y_{2t}\), can \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\) differ in sign?

MA(2): the autocovariances

Using \(\gamma_h=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_j\theta_{j+h}\) with \(\theta_0=1\):

For \(Y_{1t}\): \[\gamma_1=\sigma^2(\theta_0\theta_1+\theta_1\theta_2)=1.7\,\sigma^2,\qquad \gamma_2=\sigma^2\theta_0\theta_2=0.7\,\sigma^2.\]

For \(Y_{2t}\): \[\gamma_1=\sigma^2[-1.2+(-1.2)(0.25)]=-1.5\,\sigma^2,\qquad \gamma_2=0.25\,\sigma^2.\]

An MA(2) can display autocorrelations of different signs at lags 1 and 2; both ACFs are exactly zero for \(h>2\).

Invertibility

Invertibility

Every finite MA is covariance stationary, but different parameterizations can share the same autocovariance structure. For an MA(1), \[\rho_1=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2},\] so \(\theta\) and \(1/\theta\) give the same \(\rho_1\) (e.g. \(\theta=0.5\) and \(\theta=2\) both give \(\rho_1=0.4\)).

Invertibility picks the representation in which the innovation is recoverable from current and past observations: \[\varepsilon_t=(1+\theta L)^{-1}(Y_t-\mu)=(1-\theta L+\theta^2L^2-\cdots)(Y_t-\mu),\] which converges when \(|\theta|<1\).

Invertibility is about the uniqueness and recoverability of the innovation representation — not a stationarity condition.

R — equivalent parameterizations

equivalent_ma1 <- data.frame(theta = c(0.5, 2.0), sigma2 = c(1.0, 0.25))
equivalent_ma1$gamma0 <- equivalent_ma1$sigma2 * (1 + equivalent_ma1$theta^2)
equivalent_ma1$gamma1 <- equivalent_ma1$sigma2 * equivalent_ma1$theta
equivalent_ma1$rho1   <- equivalent_ma1$gamma1 / equivalent_ma1$gamma0
equivalent_ma1

Same \(\gamma_0,\gamma_1,\rho_1\) from two different \((\theta,\sigma^2)\) pairs — only \(|\theta|<1\) is invertible.

Root condition

For \(Y_t-\mu=(1+\theta L)\varepsilon_t\), invertibility requires the root of \(1+\theta z=0\) to lie outside the unit circle. The root is \(z=-1/\theta\), so for an MA(1), \(|\theta|<1\).

More generally, an MA(\(q\)) is invertible when every root of \[1+\theta_1z+\cdots+\theta_qz^q=0\] lies outside the unit circle.

A single coefficient larger than one does not by itself decide invertibility for MA(2)+ — the condition is on the roots of the whole lag polynomial.

R — check invertibility

ma_roots <- function(theta) {
  roots <- polyroot(c(1, theta))
  data.frame(root = roots, modulus = Mod(roots),
             outside_unit_circle = Mod(roots) > 1)
}
ma_roots(ma21)
ma_roots(ma22)

Both MA(2) examples have all roots outside the unit circle — both invertible.

Impulse responses

Wold coefficients as dynamic responses

In \(Y_t-\mu=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}\), the coefficient \(\psi_j\) is the linear response at horizon \(j\) to a one-unit innovation today.

For an MA(\(q\)) the response is exactly zero after horizon \(q\); for an infinite-order MA it may decay gradually.

These are responses to statistical innovations. A causal interpretation requires additional identification assumptions.

Predict for \(Y_t=\varepsilon_t+0.8\varepsilon_{t-1}\): the response immediately, one period later, two periods later?

Recap

Key takeaways

  • A stochastic process vs one observed realization — most properties are about the process.
  • Covariance stationarity stabilizes the law; ergodicity equates time and ensemble averages. Stationary \(\not\Rightarrow\) ergodic; dependence \(\not\Rightarrow\) non-ergodic.
  • White noise is uncorrelated, not necessarily independent; the sample ACF is only an estimate of the population ACF.
  • The Wold decomposition writes any covariance-stationary process as a linear filter of innovations; the lag operator streamlines the algebra.
  • MA(\(q\)) ACFs cut off after lag \(q\); invertibility (roots outside the unit circle) selects the recoverable innovation representation.
  • Wold coefficients \(\psi_j\) are impulse responses to statistical innovations.
