Trends and random walks

Deterministic versus stochastic trends

A deterministic trend plus a stationary disturbance is a trend-stationary (TS) process \[ Y_t=\mu+\delta t+u_t,\qquad u_t\ \text{a stationary ARMA process}. \] Shocks are temporary: \(Y_t\) keeps returning to the line \(\mu+\delta t\); detrending leaves a stationary series.

A stochastic trend accumulates past shocks — canonically, the random walk with drift \[ \Delta Y_t=\delta+\varepsilon_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Y_t=Y_0+\delta t+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_i , \] in which every innovation is permanent — it enters every future \(Y_{t+h}\) with weight one.

\(Y_t\) is not stationary but \(\Delta Y_t\) is: a difference-stationary (DS) process. More generally, DS means \(\Delta Y_t=\delta+u_t\) with \(u_t\) stationary.

The pure random walk

\(Y_t=Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), i.e. \(\Delta Y_t=\varepsilon_t\), \(\varepsilon_t\sim\text{i.i.d.}(0,\sigma^2)\). Conditional on a fixed \(Y_0\): \[ E(Y_t)=Y_0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t\sigma^2,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(Y_t,Y_{t-j})=(t-j)\sigma^2 . \] The mean is constant but the variance grows without bound — not stationary. The correlation depends on \(t\): \[ \rho_{j,t}=\frac{(t-j)\sigma^2}{\sqrt{t\sigma^2\,(t-j)\sigma^2}}=\sqrt{\frac{t-j}{t}} . \]

For fixed \(j\), \(\rho_{j,t}\to1\) as \(t\) grows; for fixed \(t\) it falls only slowly in \(j\). This is the correlogram signature of a unit root: sample autocorrelations that start near one and decline roughly linearly, not geometrically.

The sample ACF decays a little faster (it is computed around the sample mean), but the slow decay is unmistakable.

The random walk with drift

Adding a constant, \(\Delta Y_t=\delta+\varepsilon_t\), gives \[ Y_t=Y_0+\delta t+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_i, \qquad E(Y_t)=Y_0+\delta t, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t\sigma^2 . \]

A nonzero constant in the differences produces a deterministic trend in the level, while the persistence remains stochastic.

This is why the constant plays a different role in each Dickey–Fuller regression — keep it in mind for the testing strategy.

Random walk plus noise

Add measurement error \(\eta_t\sim\text{WN}(0,\sigma^2_\eta)\), independent of \(\varepsilon_t\) — the local-level model: \[ Y_t=Y_0+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\varepsilon_i+\eta_t, \qquad \Delta Y_t=\varepsilon_t+\eta_t-\eta_{t-1}. \] \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t-Y_0)=t\sigma^2+\sigma^2_\eta\), covariances \((t-j)\sigma^2\): correlations slightly below the pure random walk’s. \(\Delta Y_t\) is an MA(1) with \(\rho_1=-\sigma^2_\eta/(\sigma^2+2\sigma^2_\eta)\in(-\tfrac12,0]\): its coefficient is negative and approaches \(-1\) as \(\sigma^2_\eta/\sigma^2\) grows.

The MA root nearly cancels the unit AR root: the series looks almost stationary — exactly where unit-root tests break down.

Beveridge–Nelson (1981): every \(I(1)\) process = random walk (permanent) + stationary process (transitory).

Unit roots and integration

The unit root

AR(\(p\)): \(\phi(L)Y_t=c+\varepsilon_t\), \(\phi(z)=1-\phi_1z-\cdots-\phi_pz^p\). Stationary when all roots of \(\phi(z)=0\) lie outside the unit circle.

A unit root is a root at \(z=1\): \[ \phi(1)=1-\phi_1-\phi_2-\cdots-\phi_p=0 . \] For an AR(1) this is \(\phi=1\): the random walk. (A root at \(z=-1\) also lies on the circle but generates an oscillation at frequency \(\pi\) — a seasonal-type unit root; here “unit root” means \(z=1\).)

Factoring out the unit root

AR(2) example. With inverse roots \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\), \(\phi(L)=(1-\lambda_1L)(1-\lambda_2L)\); one unit root when \(\lambda_1=1\), \(|\lambda_2|<1\): \[ \begin{aligned} Y_t=1.5Y_{t-1}-0.5Y_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t &\;\Longleftrightarrow\; (1-L)(1-0.5L)Y_t=\varepsilon_t\\ &\;\Longleftrightarrow\; (1-0.5L)\Delta Y_t=\varepsilon_t . \end{aligned} \] Roots: \(z=1\) (on the circle) and \(z=2\) (outside). A double unit root, \((1-L)^2Y_t=\varepsilon_t\), must be differenced twice.

A single unit root factors as \(\phi(L)=(1-L)\phi^\ast(L)\), with \(\phi^\ast\) of order \(p-1\) and stationary, so \(\Delta Y_t=c+\phi^\ast_1\Delta Y_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi^\ast_{p-1}\Delta Y_{t-p+1}+\varepsilon_t\) is a stationary AR(\(p-1\)) in the differences.

Shocks become permanent

Write the differences in Wold form, \(\Delta Y_t=\delta+\psi^\ast(L)\varepsilon_t\) with \(\psi^\ast(L)=\beta(L)/\phi^\ast(L)\). The response of the level is the cumulated response of the difference: \[ \frac{\partial Y_{t+h}}{\partial\varepsilon_t}=\sum_{j=0}^{h}\psi^\ast_j \;\longrightarrow\; \psi^\ast(1)=\frac{\beta(1)}{\phi^\ast(1)}\qquad(h\to\infty). \]

\(\psi^\ast(1)\) is the long-run impact of a shock — Campbell and Mankiw (1987) use it to measure the persistence of output fluctuations. For the AR(2) above, \(\psi^\ast(1)=1/(1-0.5)=2\): a unit shock eventually raises \(Y\) by two units, permanently.

\[\boxed{\text{stationary: }\lim_{h\to\infty}\partial Y_{t+h}/\partial\varepsilon_t=0},\qquad \boxed{\text{unit root: }\lim_{h\to\infty}\partial Y_{t+h}/\partial\varepsilon_t=\psi^\ast(1)\neq0}.\]

Integration and ARIMA models

A process that must be differenced \(d\) times to become stationary (and invertible) is integrated of order \(d\), \(Y_t\sim I(d)\). Stationary series are \(I(0)\); random walks are \(I(1)\).

If \(\Delta^dY_t\) is a stationary ARMA(\(p,q\)), \(Y_t\) is an ARIMA(\(p,d,q\)) process: \[ \phi(L)(1-L)^dY_t=c+\beta(L)\varepsilon_t . \] ARIMA(1,1,0) is \(\Delta Y_t=c+\phi\Delta Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) — the AR(2) example with \(c=0\) and \(\phi=0.5\).

In R, arima(y, order = c(p, d, q)) differences internally, but with \(d\ge1\) it drops the constant. Add a drift as a regressor (xreg = seq_along(y)) or fit the model to diff(y).

One model that nests the cases

The component form \(Y_t=\mu+\delta t+u_t\), \(u_t=\phi u_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), nests TS and DS. Substituting \(u_t=Y_t-\mu-\delta t\) into the AR(1) gives the reduced form \[ \boxed{\;Y_t=\underbrace{\mu(1-\phi)+\phi\delta}_{\text{constant}}\;+\;\underbrace{\delta(1-\phi)}_{\text{trend coefficient}}\,t\;+\;\phi Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t .\;} \]

  • \(|\phi|<1\): trend-stationary (\(\delta\neq0\)) or stationary around \(\mu\) (\(\delta=0\)).
  • \(\phi=1\): the trend coefficient vanishes and the constant becomes \(\delta\), so \(\Delta Y_t=\delta+\varepsilon_t\) — a random walk with drift (pure random walk if \(\delta=0\)).

Under the unit-root null the deterministic terms are restricted — so the \(\Phi\) tests test them jointly with \(\phi=1\).

Detrend or difference?

Stochastic trend → difference; deterministic trend → detrend. The wrong choice is costly in both directions.

Over-differencing a TS series. If \(Y_t=\mu+\delta t+\varepsilon_t\), then \(\Delta Y_t=\delta+\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1}\): an MA(1) with coefficient \(-1\), a unit root in the MA polynomial. Stationary but not invertible; \(\rho_1=-0.5\) and the PACF decays slowly. Differencing a stationary AR(1) gives the ARMA(1,1) \(\Delta Y_t=\phi\Delta Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t-\varepsilon_{t-1}\): needless structure that hurts identification and forecasts.

Detrending a DS series. Regressing a random walk on time does not remove its stochastic trend. The residuals remain \(I(1)\) and show long spurious “cycles” (Nelson and Kang 1981).

Any business-cycle measure obtained by linearly detrending a series with a stochastic trend is suspect.

Why the \(t\)-test fails under a unit root

OLS when \(\phi=1\)

Take \(Y_t=\phi Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), \(Y_0=0\), and \[ \hat\phi=\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}Y_tY_{t-1}}{\sum_{t=2}^{T}Y_{t-1}^2}. \] When \(|\phi|<1\), Lecture 9 gave \(\sqrt T(\hat\phi-\phi)\overset{d}{\to}N(0,\,1-\phi^2)\). At \(\phi=1\) that variance is zero — a first hint that something changes.

Because \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_t)=t\sigma^2\), the denominator grows like \(T^2\), not \(T\), and (Phillips 1987) \[ T(\hat\phi-1)\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{\tfrac12\{W(1)^2-1\}}{\int_0^1W(r)^2\,dr}, \qquad \tau=\frac{\hat\phi-1}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\phi)}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{\tfrac12\{W(1)^2-1\}}{\big\{\int_0^1W(r)^2\,dr\big\}^{1/2}}, \] where \(W(r)\) is a standard Brownian motion, the continuous-time limit of a scaled random walk.

Four consequences

  1. Superconsistency. \(\hat\phi\) converges at rate \(T\), not \(\sqrt T\).
  2. Non-normal, skewed limits. \(W(1)^2\sim\chi^2(1)\) and \(P\{\chi^2(1)<1\}\approx0.68\), so about two-thirds of estimates fall below one even though \(\phi=1\). OLS is biased downwards, and \(\tau\) lies to the left of \(N(0,1)\).
  3. Deterministic terms matter. A constant (or trend) replaces \(W\) by demeaned (detrended) Brownian motion: the distribution moves further left; each case needs its own critical values.
  4. Simulated critical values. No closed form: Dickey and Fuller (1979) and Fuller (1976) tabulated them; MacKinnon (1996) response surfaces give them, and \(p\)-values, for any \(T\).

Dickey–Fuller tests and a testing strategy

The Dickey–Fuller regressions

Subtract \(Y_{t-1}\): \(\Delta Y_t=\gamma Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), \(\gamma=\phi-1\); test \(H_0:\gamma=0\) vs \(H_1:\gamma<0\). The \(t\)-ratio \(\hat\gamma/\operatorname{SE}(\hat\gamma)\) (identical to \((\hat\phi-1)/\operatorname{SE}(\hat\phi)\)) is compared with Dickey–Fuller, not normal, critical values. \[ \begin{aligned} &\text{none:} && \Delta Y_t=\gamma Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\\ &\text{drift:} && \Delta Y_t=c+\gamma Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\\ &\text{trend:} && \Delta Y_t=c+\delta t+\gamma Y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t \end{aligned} \] Dickey and Fuller (1981) add \(F\)-type statistics for joint hypotheses on deterministic terms and \(\gamma\): \[ \Phi=\frac{(\text{RSS}_R-\text{RSS}_U)/r}{\text{RSS}_U/(T-k)}, \] with \(r\) restrictions and \(k\) parameters in the unrestricted regression.

Choosing the deterministic terms

Think about \(H_1\) as well as \(H_0\) — both should fit what the data could be (Hamilton 1994, §17.4; Elder and Kennedy 2001).

drift RW without drift (\(c=0\)) stationary, nonzero mean no trend, nonzero level
trend RW with drift trend-stationary trend
  • Omitting a term that is present makes the test inconsistent: a TS series tested with the drift regression almost never rejects, however large \(T\).
  • Including a term that is absent costs power: each extra term moves the critical value left.

For a trending series, start with the trend regression — under the alternative only it describes the data.

The augmented Dickey–Fuller test

Autocorrelated errors invalidate the DF distribution. Any AR(\(p\)) can be written \[ \Delta Y_t=c+\gamma Y_{t-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\alpha_i\,\Delta Y_{t-i}+\varepsilon_t, \qquad \gamma=-\phi(1)=\phi_1+\cdots+\phi_p-1, \qquad \alpha_i=-\sum_{j=i+1}^{p}\phi_j , \] — a unit root is again \(\gamma=0\). E.g. for an AR(3): \[ \Delta Y_t=(\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3-1)Y_{t-1}-(\phi_2+\phi_3)\Delta Y_{t-1}-\phi_3\Delta Y_{t-2}+\varepsilon_t . \] The ADF regression adds lagged differences to the chosen deterministic terms: \[ \Delta Y_t=c+\delta t+\gamma Y_{t-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_i\,\Delta Y_{t-i}+\varepsilon_t . \]

ADF critical values and the lag length

\(\tau\) and \(\Phi\) keep the same critical values. With ARMA errors, ADF stays valid if \(p\) grows with \(T\), no faster than \(T^{1/3}\) (Said and Dickey 1984).

Choosing \(p\).

  • Set a maximum, e.g. Schwert’s (1989) rule \(p_{\max}=\lfloor12(T/100)^{1/4}\rfloor\).
  • Go general-to-specific — drop the last lag while insignificant (Ng and Perron 1995) — or minimize AIC/BIC over \(p\le p_{\max}\) on a common sample.
  • Check the residuals are white noise (Ljung–Box, Lecture 9).

Too few lags leave autocorrelation and distort the size; too many waste degrees of freedom and reduce power.

More than one unit root

If \(I(2)\) is possible, test from the top down (Dickey and Pantula 1987): first test for a unit root in \(\Delta Y_t\), \[ \Delta^2Y_t=c+\gamma\,\Delta Y_{t-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_i\,\Delta^2Y_{t-i}+\varepsilon_t , \] and only if that is rejected test \(Y_t\) itself.

Top-down (Dickey and Pantula 1987): testing \(Y_t\) first assumes there is at most one unit root.

\(I(2)\) is rare in economics; nominal price levels and money stocks are sometimes candidates.

The sequential procedure (1/2)

The true deterministic terms are unknown, and both omitting and including them have costs. Start from the most general plausible model and simplify only when the data allow (Dolado, Jenkinson and Sosvilla-Rivero 1990; Enders 2015, Ch. 4):

  1. Trend regression. Estimate \(\Delta Y_t=c+\delta t+\gamma Y_{t-1}+\sum\alpha_i\Delta Y_{t-i}+\varepsilon_t\); test \(\gamma=0\) with \(\tau_\tau\). Rejected → \(Y_t\) is (trend-)stationary: stop; ordinary \(t\)-tests on \(c\), \(\delta\) are now valid.
  2. Is the trend needed? If \(\tau_\tau\) does not reject, test \(\delta=\gamma=0\) with \(\Phi_3\). If \(\Phi_3\) rejects, the trend belongs: test \(\gamma=0\) again with the standard normal (with a trend in the DGP the \(t\)-ratio is asymptotically normal; West 1988). Still not rejected → unit root with a trend in the differences: stop.
  3. Drift regression. If \(\Phi_3\) does not reject, drop the trend and test with \(\tau_\mu\). Rejects → stationary around a mean: stop.

The sequential procedure (2/2)

  1. Is the drift needed? If \(\tau_\mu\) does not reject, test \(c=\gamma=0\) with \(\Phi_1\). If \(\Phi_1\) rejects, the drift belongs: test \(\gamma=0\) with the standard normal. Non-rejection → random walk with drift: stop.
  2. No deterministic terms. If \(\Phi_1\) does not reject, estimate \(\Delta Y_t=\gamma Y_{t-1}+\cdots\) and test with \(\tau\). Non-rejection → random walk without drift.
  3. Confirm the order of integration by testing \(\Delta Y_t\) the same way. Rejecting a unit root in \(\Delta Y_t\) confirms \(Y_t\sim I(1)\).

You rarely need every step: a plot, and economic knowledge — does the series grow? — can replace a low-powered pre-test (Elder and Kennedy 2001).

Knowing why each step is there — what the series looks like under \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) — matters more than following the chart.

Confirmatory tests, size and power

Phillips–Perron

ADF handles serial correlation parametrically (add lags). Phillips and Perron (1988) estimate the non-augmented DF regression and correct its \(t\)-ratio non-parametrically: \[ Z_\tau=\frac{\hat\sigma}{\hat\lambda}\,\tau-\frac{\hat\lambda^2-\hat\sigma^2}{2\hat\lambda}\cdot\frac{T\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat\gamma)}{s}, \] \(\hat\sigma^2\) the residual variance, \(s\) the regression standard error, \(\hat\lambda^2\) a Newey–West estimate of the long-run variance \(\lambda^2=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma_j\). With white-noise errors \(\lambda^2=\sigma^2\) and \(Z_\tau=\tau\).

Under \(H_0\), \(Z_\tau\) has the same limit as \(\tau\) for the same deterministic terms: the DF critical values apply. PP is robust to general serial correlation and heteroskedasticity.

PP is badly over-sized when the errors have a large negative MA root (Schwert 1989) — see the size experiment below.

KPSS: reversing the hypotheses

Kwiatkowski, Phillips, Schmidt and Shin (1992): \[ Y_t=\delta t+r_t+u_t,\qquad r_t=r_{t-1}+\eta_t,\qquad \eta_t\sim\text{WN}(0,\sigma^2_\eta), \] \(u_t\) stationary. \(H_0:\sigma^2_\eta=0\) — \(r_t\) is a constant and \(Y_t\) is stationary around a level (\(\delta=0\)) or a trend. If \(\sigma^2_\eta>0\), \(Y_t\) has a unit root.

With \(\hat e_t\) the residuals from regressing \(Y_t\) on a constant (or constant and trend) and partial sums \(\hat S_t=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\hat e_j\): \[ \text{KPSS}=\frac{1}{T^2\hat\lambda^2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat S_t^{\,2}. \]

Under stationarity the partial sums stay small; under a unit root they wander. Right-tailed: 5% critical values 0.463 (level) and 0.146 (trend).

Size and power: the two notorious problems

  • Low power. A stationary AR(1) with \(\phi=0.95\) differs from a random walk only in the long run, and \(T=100\) carries little information about the long run. Superfluous deterministic terms lower power further.
  • Size distortions. When \(\Delta Y_t\) has a large negative MA root (random walk plus noise; an over-differenced series), the MA root nearly cancels the AR unit root and tests reject a true unit root far too often (Schwert 1989).

\[\boxed{\text{no rejection}\not\Rightarrow\text{unit root}}\ \text{(low power)}\] \[\boxed{\text{rejection}\not\Rightarrow\text{no unit root}}\ \text{(negative MA roots)}\]

Practical lessons

  1. Span beats frequency. Power depends on the length of time covered, not the number of observations: 30 years of monthly data carry little more information about mean reversion than 30 annual observations (Shiller and Perron 1985).
  2. Lag selection matters most when MA roots are large. BIC tends to choose too few lags; AIC more; Ng and Perron’s (2001) modified AIC was designed for this problem.
  3. Seasonality. Seasonal dummies for deterministic seasonality; seasonal unit-root tests (Hylleberg, Engle, Granger and Yoo 1990) otherwise. Seasonal-adjustment filters can bias tests towards non-rejection (Ghysels and Perron 1993).
  4. Breaks in the level or slope of a trend make a stationary series look like a unit root.
