Definitions

Sample space, outcomes, events

  • A sample space \(S\) is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment.
  • An outcome is one element \(s\in S\); an event is a subset \(A\subseteq S\).

Sample space, outcomes, events

Example. Let \(M\) = number of times the internet fails during a lecture: \[S=\{0,1,2,3,4,\ldots\}.\] “The connection fails at most once” is the event \(A=\{0,1\}\).

The probability of an event \(A\), written \(P(A)\), captures the idea of chance.

Kolmogorov’s three axioms

  1. Non-negativity: \(P(A)\ge 0\) for any \(A\subseteq S\).
  2. Normalization: \(P(S)=1\).
  3. Additivity: if \(A\cap B=\varnothing\), then \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\).

Kolmogorov’s three axioms

General addition rule (events need not be disjoint): \[P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B),\]

where \(P(A\cap B)\) is the joint probability. - If \(A,B\) are disjoint, \(P(A\cap B)=0\) and this reduces to Axiom 3.

Conditional probability & Bayes

Conditional probability

\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0.\]

Example. \(30\%\) of students play football, \(10\%\) play both football and cricket: \[P(F)=0.30,\quad P(F\cap C)=0.10.\] What is the probability that a football player also plays cricket?

Conditional probability

Ans: \[P(C\mid F)=\frac{P(C\cap F)}{P(F)}=\frac{0.10}{0.30}=\tfrac{1}{3}.\]

Law of total probability

If \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\) are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, then \[P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i).\]

This lets us assemble an overall probability from conditional pieces — exactly what we need to normalize Bayes’ theorem.

Bayes’ theorem

\[P(B_j\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}.\]

  • \(P(B_j)\) — the prior
  • \(P(A\mid B_j)\) — probability of the evidence under \(B_j\)
  • \(P(B_j\mid A)\) — the posterior (updated belief)

Idea. Bayes’ theorem is a rule for updating a prior belief in light of evidence. The word likelihood will be defined precisely when we reach estimation.

Example — a medical test

  • \(P(D)=0.01\) — prevalence
  • \(P(+\mid D)=0.99\) — sensitivity
  • \(P(+\mid \neg D)=0.05\) — false-positive rate

\[P(+)=0.99(0.01)+0.05(0.99)=0.0594\] \[P(D\mid +)=\frac{0.99\cdot 0.01}{0.0594}\approx 0.167\]

The base-rate lesson. Even a highly accurate test gives only a ~16.7% chance of disease after a positive result — because the disease is rare. Most positives are false positives drawn from the large healthy group.

Example 2 — contact tracing

Of 59 traced patients: 44 knew their infection source \((A)\), 15 did not \((A')\). \[P(A)=\tfrac{44}{59}\approx 0.75,\qquad P(A')=\tfrac{15}{59}\approx 0.25.\]

Self-quarantine \((Q)\): 43 of the 44 with a known source quarantined; 6 of the 15 with unknown source did not. \[P(Q\mid A)=\tfrac{43}{44}\approx 0.98,\qquad P(Q\mid A')=1-\tfrac{6}{15}=0.6.\]

Given a patient quarantined, what is the chance their source was unknown?

Example 2 — contact tracing

Ans:

\[P(Q)=\tfrac{43}{59}+\tfrac{9}{59}=\tfrac{52}{59}\approx 0.881,\qquad \boxed{P(A'\mid Q)=\frac{9/59}{52/59}=\frac{9}{52}\approx 0.173.}\]

Your turn — three Bayes exercises

1 · Credit risk

A bank lends to Low-risk(L) and High-risk(H) borrowers: \(P(L)=0.70,\ P(H)=0.30\).

Their default probs: \(P(D\mid L)=0.02,\ P(D\mid H)=0.10\).

Find \(P(D)\) and the posterior \(P(H\mid D)\).

2 · Product returns

A firm sells in two regions : Urban(U) and Rural (R) \(P(U)=0.60,\ P(R\mid U)=0.05\);

\(P(Ru)=0.40,\ P(R\mid Ru)=0.12\).

Find \(P(R)\) and \(P(Ru\mid R)\).

3 · Rate forecast

The central bank raises rates with probability \(P(H)=0.30\);

Analyst A’s forecasting performance: \(P(\text{pred }H\mid H)=0.80\), \(P(\text{pred }H\mid H')=0.20\).

Find \(P(H\mid \text{pred }H)\).

For each: draw the probability tree (split by type, then by outcome), get the overall probability by the law of total probability, then invert with Bayes’ theorem.

Random variables & distributions

Random variables

A random variable is a numerical summary of a random outcome.

  • Discrete RV — countably many values, each with a probability.
  • Continuous RV — a continuum of values (e.g. tomorrow’s temperature).

Random variables

The pmf lists values and probabilities;

the CDF gives \(\Pr(Y\le y)\).

Probability that your connection fails \(M\) times
Outcome \(m\) 0 1 2 3 4
pmf \(\Pr(M=m)\) 0.80 0.10 0.06 0.03 0.01
CDF \(\Pr(M\le m)\) 0.80 0.90 0.96 0.99 1.00

For a continuous RV, probability is a pdf: \(\Pr(a\le Y\le b)=\int_a^b f(y)\,dy\).

Shape of a distribution

Expected value & variance

Expected value (mean) — the long-run average: \[E[Y]=\sum_i y_i p_i=\mu \quad(\text{discrete}),\qquad E[Y]=\int y\,f(y)\,dy=\mu \quad(\text{continuous}).\] Linearity: \(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\) and \(E[aX+b]=aE[X]+b\).

Variance & covariance: \[\mathrm{Var}(Y)=E[(Y-\mu)^2]=E[Y^2]-(E[Y])^2=\sigma^2.\] \[\mathrm{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X),\] \[\qquad \mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y).\] \[\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-EX)(Y-EY)]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y].\]

Moments, skewness, kurtosis

  • Raw \(r\)-th moment: \(\mu'_r=E[Y^r]\).
  • Central \(r\)-th moment: \(\mu_r=E[(Y-\mu)^r]\).
  • Standardized \(r\)-th moment: \(\gamma_r=\mu_r/\sigma^r\).

Moments, skewness, kurtosis

\[\text{Skew}(Y)=\gamma_3=\frac{\mu_3}{\sigma^3},\qquad \beta_2=\frac{\mu_4}{\sigma^4},\]

\[\text{Excess kurtosis}=\beta_2-3.\]

Skewness measures asymmetry.

Excess kurtosis measures heavy tails relative to the normal (which has \(\beta_2=3\)). Financial returns are typically leptokurtic — heavy-tailed.

Joint, marginal & conditional distributions

One joint distribution throughout

\(X\) and \(Y\) are binary variables representing two weather conditions: very cold and rain

Joint distribution of \(X\) and \(Y\)
Very cold \((X=1)\) Not very cold \((X=0)\) Total
Rain \((Y=1)\) 0.15 0.07 0.22
No rain \((Y=0)\) 0.15 0.63 0.78
Total 0.30 0.70 1.00

Marginal — sum over the other variable: \(\ \Pr(Y=1)=0.15+0.07=0.22.\)

Conditional — \(\ \Pr(Y=1\mid X=1)=\dfrac{0.15}{0.30}=0.5.\)

Conditional expectation

Conditional expectation: \[E(Y\mid X=x)=\sum_i y_i\,\Pr(Y=y_i\mid X=x),\] \[E(Y\mid X=1)=1(0.5)+0(0.5)=0.5.\]

Law of iterated expectations

Law of iterated expectations — the unconditional mean is the average of conditional means: \[E[Y]=E\big[\,E(Y\mid X)\,\big]=\sum_x E(Y\mid X=x)\,\Pr(X=x).\]

Why this matters for time series. \(E(Y\mid X)\) is the best predictor of \(Y\) given \(X\) in the mean-squared-error sense. Conditional expectations are forecasts — which is why they sit at the centre of the course.

Independence, covariance, correlation

Independence: \(\ \Pr(Y=y\mid X=x)=\Pr(Y=y) \qquad \Longrightarrow\ \Pr(X=x,Y=y)=\Pr(X=x)\Pr(Y=y).\)

Mean independence: \(\ E(Y\mid X)=E(Y)\) for all \(X\).

Covariance: \(\ \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\).

For the table above, \(=0.15-0.30(0.22)=0.084.\)

Independence, covariance, correlation

Correlation (unit-free): \(\ \mathrm{Corr}(X,Y)=\dfrac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)\mathrm{Var}(Y)}}\in[-1,1].\)

  • Independent \(\Rightarrow\) mean independent \(\Rightarrow\) uncorrelated.
  • Uncorrelated does not imply independence — correlation captures only linear co-movement.

R demo — zero correlation, clear dependence

Generate \(X\sim N(0,1)\) and \(Y=X^2+u,\ u\sim N(0,0.2^2)\).

The link is nonlinear, so correlation misses it.

Populations, samples & sampling distributions

The vocabulary of inference

  • A population is the collection of possible observations described by a probability model. A feature of it — \(\mu,\ \sigma^2,\ \rho\) — is a parameter.
  • A sample is the observed data \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n\).
  • A statistic is a function of the sample with no unknown parameters.
  • An estimator is a statistic used to estimate a parameter; its realized value is an estimate.

\[\bar Y=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i \quad\text{is an estimator of } \mu.\]

The vocabulary of inference

Random sampling — iid: \(\ Y_1,\ldots,Y_n\overset{iid}{\sim}f(y\mid\theta)\): same marginal distribution, mutually independent.

Time-series observations generally violate the independence part of iid.

Sampling distribution & standard error

Because an estimator is a function of random data, it has a distribution across repeated samples — its sampling distribution.

The standard deviation of an estimator’s sampling distribution is its standard error.

LLN and CLT

For iid \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) with mean \(\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2\), and \(\bar Y_n=\frac1n\sum Y_i\):

Law of Large Numbers. \(\ \bar Y_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu\) as \(n\to\infty\) — averages converge to the truth. This is why estimators can be consistent.

Central Limit Theorem. Whatever the shape of the population, \[\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar Y_n-\mu}{\sigma}\ \xrightarrow{d}\ N(0,1).\]

See the CLT happen

Sampling distributions in R

An Example

A food-delivery company wants the average delivery time \(\mu\) in a city.

  • Population: all possible deliveries under similar conditions (\(\mu\) is a parameter).
  • Sample: they record \(n=30\) deliveries, \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_{30}\).
  • \(\bar Y=\frac{1}{30}\sum Y_i\) is an estimator of \(\mu\); its value is an estimate.

An Example

Delivery times are right-skewed (a few very long trips). Still, the LLN makes \(\bar Y\) approach \(\mu\), and the CLT makes \(\bar Y\) approximately normal.

If deliveries are recorded consecutively — same rush hour, same rainstorm — iid is questionable. That is precisely the time-series situation.

Step 1 — build a known population

We construct a large artificial population so the parameters are known (in real world they never are).

set.seed(821)
N <- 100000                     # size of simulated population
shape <- 4; scale <- 5          # gamma parameters

population <- rgamma(N, shape = shape, scale = scale)

mu    <- shape * scale                 # theoretical mean = 20
sigma <- sqrt(shape * scale^2)         # theoretical sd   = 10

round(c(theoretical_mean = mu,
        simulated_mean   = mean(population),
        theoretical_sd   = sigma,
        simulated_sd     = sd(population)), 2)

The simulated values sit close to the theoretical mean (20 min) and sd (10 min) because the population is large.

The population is right-skewed

hist(population, breaks = 60, probability = TRUE,
     main = "Population distribution of delivery times",
     xlab = "Delivery time (minutes)", col = "grey85", border = "white")
abline(v = mean(population), col = "red", lwd = 2)

Step 2 — one random sample

Record only \(n=30\) deliveries and estimate \(\mu\) by the sample mean.

set.seed(123)
n <- 30
sample_delivery_times <- sample(population, size = n, replace = FALSE)
round(mean(sample_delivery_times), 2)

The sample mean is not exactly \(\mu\): a different 30 deliveries would give a different mean. That variability is sampling variation — one sample gives one estimate.

Step 3 — repeat the study 5000 times

Repeating the whole study and collecting the means traces out the sampling distribution of \(\bar Y\).

set.seed(456)
B <- 5000; n <- 30
sample_means_30 <- replicate(B, mean(sample(population, size = n, replace = FALSE)))

hist(sample_means_30, breaks = 40, probability = TRUE,
     main = "Sampling distribution of the sample mean", sub = "n = 30",
     xlab = "Mean delivery time", col = "grey85", border = "white")
abline(v = mean(population), col = "red", lwd = 2)
curve(dnorm(x, mean = mean(population), sd = sd(population)/sqrt(n)),
      add = TRUE, col = "blue", lwd = 2, lty = 2)
legend("topright", legend = c("Population mean", "Normal approx."),
       col = c("red","blue"), lty = c(1,2), lwd = 2, bty = "n")

From skew to bell

Although individual times are right-skewed, the distribution of \(\bar Y\) is approximately bell-shaped and centred at \(\mu\). Its spread is the standard error:

\[SE(\bar Y)\approx\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad \frac{10}{\sqrt{30}}\approx 1.83\text{ minutes.}\]

This is how much sample means typically differ across repeated samples.

Step 4 — vary the sample size

Same population, same 5000 repetitions — only \(n\) changes.

set.seed(789)
B <- 5000; sample_sizes <- c(5, 30, 100, 500)

sampling_distributions <- lapply(sample_sizes, function(n)
  replicate(B, mean(sample(population, size = n, replace = FALSE))))

common_xlim <- range(unlist(sampling_distributions))
par(mfrow = c(2, 2), mar = c(4, 4, 3, 1))
for (n in sample_sizes) {
  means_n <- sampling_distributions[[which(sample_sizes == n)]]
  se_n <- sd(population)/sqrt(n) * sqrt((N - n)/(N - 1))   # finite-pop. correction
  hist(means_n, breaks = 40, probability = TRUE, xlim = common_xlim,
       main = paste0("n = ", n, ";  SE = ", round(sd(means_n), 2)),
       xlab = "Sample mean", col = "grey85", border = "white")
  abline(v = mean(population), col = "red", lwd = 2)
  curve(dnorm(x, mean = mean(population), sd = se_n),
        add = TRUE, col = "blue", lwd = 2, lty = 2)
}
par(mfrow = c(1, 1))

Three results to remember

  1. The centre barely moves. Every sampling distribution is centred near \(\mu=20\). Larger \(n\) does not systematically shift \(\bar Y\) — the estimator is unbiased.
  2. The spread shrinks. Since \(SE(\bar Y)\approx \sigma/\sqrt n\), larger samples give more precise estimates.
  3. The shape becomes normal. Skewed at \(n=5\), bell-shaped by \(n=30\), sharply normal at \(n=100,500\) — the CLT at work.

Where this leads

The bridge to time series

Every classical result today leaned on one assumption: independence.

Concept iid world (today) Time-series world (the course)
Dependence none serial dependence is the object of study
Best forecast \(E(Y)\) \(E(Y_t \mid \text{past})\)
LLN / CLT classical ergodic / weak-dependence versions
“Uncorrelated” often “independent enough” can still be strongly dependent (GARCH)

Keep two tools close: Bayes’ theorem (updating beliefs) and the sampling distribution (quantifying estimator uncertainty). Everything ahead builds on them.

Recap

  • Probability obeys Kolmogorov’s axioms; Bayes’ theorem updates a prior into a posterior — and base rates dominate.
  • A distribution is summarised by its moments: mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis.
  • Conditional expectation is the best predictor — the seed of forecasting.
  • Uncorrelated \(\neq\) independent; correlation sees only linear structure.
  • An estimator has a sampling distribution; its spread is the standard error.
  • LLN + CLT justify inference — if observations are independent, which time series are not.
